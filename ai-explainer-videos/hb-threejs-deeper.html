Three.js, in more depth

The scene graph, cameras, lights, materials, animation loops and performance for explainer scenes.

Three.js is a library for drawing 3D graphics in the browser using WebGL. Step 12 builds our standalone 3D airplane scene and step 13 moves it inside Remotion. This chapter fills in the concepts those steps use: the scene graph, the perspective camera, the lights, the standard material, the animation loop, and how @remotion/three ties frame-based animation to react-three-fiber. All of it is consistent with the three 0.182.0 this project renders with.

The scene graph: a tree of things in space

Everything you draw lives in a scene, and the scene is a tree. Each node has a position, a rotation and a scale, and those transforms add up down the tree, so moving a parent moves all its children together. This tree is the scene graph. In our airplane, the fuselage, nose, wings, tail and fin are separate meshes grouped under one parent, so a single move or tilt of the group carries the whole plane at once.

Meshes, geometry and material

A mesh is the pairing of a shape and a surface. The shape is a geometry: the vertices that define a box, a cylinder, a cone or a sphere. The surface is a material: how that shape responds to light and what colour it is. Our plane is built entirely from primitive geometries, no model files at all: a cylinder fuselage, a cone nose, box wings and tail, and spheres clustered into clouds.

const body = new THREE.Mesh(
  new THREE.CylinderGeometry(0.28, 0.28, 2.4, 24),
  new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({ color: "#e5e7eb", roughness: 0.5, metalness: 0.2 })
);
New to 3D coordinates? What x, y and z mean

A position in 3D is a vector of three numbers: x goes right, y goes up, z comes toward you. A rotation is three angles in radians, so a right angle is written as Pi over two. Our fuselage cylinder stands upright by default, so the code rotates it a quarter turn to lie along the plane's length. Getting comfortable with these three axes is most of what 3D asks of a beginner.

The camera: a perspective view

Nothing is visible until a camera looks at the scene. The usual choice for a lifelike view is a perspective camera, where distant things appear smaller, just as in real life. You create it with four numbers: the field of view in degrees, the aspect ratio, and a near and far clipping distance. Anything closer than near or farther than far is not drawn. Our scene uses a 55 degree field of view and places the camera slightly up and back from the plane.

const camera = new THREE.PerspectiveCamera(55, 16 / 9, 0.1, 100);
camera.position.set(0, 1, 7);

Lights: shape comes from lighting

A standard material shows nothing without light. Two kinds cover most explainer scenes. An ambient light raises the overall brightness evenly, so no face is fully black; it has no direction. A directional light acts like the sun, coming from one direction and giving surfaces their bright and shaded sides, which is what makes a shape read as solid. Our scene uses a soft ambient fill plus one directional sun from the upper right.

scene.add(new THREE.AmbientLight(0xffffff, 1.1));   // even fill, no direction
const sun = new THREE.DirectionalLight(0xffffff, 1.6);
sun.position.set(5, 6, 5);                          // light comes from here
scene.add(sun);

The animation loop

A still render shows one moment. To animate, you redraw many times per second, nudging things a little each time. In a plain browser scene this is a loop driven by requestAnimationFrame, which asks the browser to call your draw function before the next screen refresh. Each call computes the current time, updates positions, and renders once.

function loop() {
  renderAt((performance.now() - start) / 1000);
  requestAnimationFrame(loop);
}

Our standalone scene writes its movement as a function of time, renderAt(t), so the very same time value can either come from the live clock during the loop or be frozen for a single frame. That design is what lets a ?t=SECONDS URL capture one exact frame for a screenshot, and it is the bridge to rendering inside Remotion.

Three.js inside Remotion with react-three-fiber

react-three-fiber lets you build a Three.js scene as React elements instead of imperative calls: a <mesh> with a <boxGeometry> and a <meshStandardMaterial> inside it describes the same mesh as the code above. @remotion/three provides a ThreeCanvas that is a react-three-fiber canvas wired to Remotion's frame clock. The crucial change is that there is no requestAnimationFrame loop: the frame is driven by useCurrentFrame, so every frame is computed deterministically and records cleanly to MP4.

<mesh position={[0, 0, 0]}>
  <boxGeometry args={[0.7, 0.06, 3.4]} />
  <meshStandardMaterial color="#60a5fa" />
</mesh>
WebGL in a headless render Rendering a 3D composition needs WebGL to work inside Remotion's headless Chrome, which is why step 15 adds the --gl=angle flag. Without it the 3D frames can come out blank. The 2D composition does not need it.

Performance tips for explainer scenes

Explainer scenes are small, so performance is rarely a problem, but a few habits keep renders fast and clean. Reuse a material across meshes that share a look rather than creating a new one each time, as our clouds do with one shared white material. Keep geometry simple: a sphere with a modest segment count looks perfectly round at explainer scale and costs far less than a highly detailed one. Prefer primitives over imported models when primitives will do, because they load instantly and have no external files. And remember the camera's far distance clips anything beyond it, so there is no cost to things you have placed out of view.

Sensible defaults we used
  • Primitive geometries only, grouped so the plane moves as a unit.
  • One ambient light plus one directional light for a solid, readable look.
  • A single shared material for the repeated cloud spheres.
  • Movement written as a function of time, so preview and render agree.
Recap
  • The scene graph is a tree; transforms inherit, so grouping lets you move many parts together.
  • A mesh is a geometry (shape) plus a material (surface); our plane is all primitives.
  • A perspective camera needs a field of view, an aspect ratio and near and far distances.
  • Ambient light fills evenly; a directional light gives shape its lit and shaded sides.
  • Browser scenes animate with a requestAnimationFrame loop; @remotion/three uses the frame clock instead for deterministic renders.
  • Keep geometry simple, reuse materials, and favour primitives for fast, clean explainer scenes.
