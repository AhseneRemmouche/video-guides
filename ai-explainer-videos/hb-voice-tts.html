Voice and text-to-speech

How TTS works, writing voice descriptions and per-line expressions, and directing a natural read.

The voice-over is the spine of an AI explainer. It sets the pace, the mood and the length of the finished video, and every scene is timed to land on the line it illustrates. This chapter is the background reading for step 4: how text-to-speech turns written lines into a spoken recording, how to write a voice description and per-line expression cues, how to choose a voice, and how to direct a read that sounds like a person rather than a robot.

How text-to-speech works, at a high level

A modern TTS system takes written text and produces audio of a voice reading it. You do not record anyone; the voice is generated. The tool used in the video is Google's Gemini Flash TTS, reached through Google AI Studio. The important idea for a beginner is that today's TTS is controllable: alongside the words, you can give it a description of the voice you want and a note of how each line should be delivered. The model uses all of that to shape pitch, speed, warmth and emphasis.

New to TTS? What "generated" voice means

Older TTS stitched together small recorded fragments and sounded flat. Newer systems are neural: a model that has learned from a great deal of speech generates the whole waveform, so it can carry rhythm, emphasis and emotion. That is why a description like "warm storyteller, slightly slow" actually changes the result, and why a cue like "excited" on one line lifts that line. You are not editing an audio file; you are briefing a performer who then performs once.

The two layers of direction: description and expression

There are two separate controls, and keeping them straight is the key skill. The voice description applies to the whole narration: who this narrator is and how they generally sound. The expression is attached to each line and says how that one line should be delivered. Claude returns both when you use the script prompt, so you usually start from its suggestions and adjust.

Writing a voice description

A good description names a few concrete qualities rather than piling on adjectives. Think about who is speaking (a teacher, a storyteller, a documentary narrator), the pace (slow, measured, lively), the warmth (gentle, upbeat, serious) and the audience it is pitched to. The creator chose a storyteller-style voice for the children's airplane video, which suits simple images and a calm pace. A compact description such as the one below is enough to steer the model:

Voice: a warm, friendly storyteller reading to a young child.
Pace is gentle and unhurried. Clear, simple, a little playful.
Never shouty; smiles through the words.

Writing per-line expressions

Expressions are short delivery notes, one per line, matching the feeling of that sentence. A question can be curious, a surprising fact can be amazed, a resolution can be reassuring. Keep them to a word or two and let them follow the natural arc of the story, so the read rises and falls instead of droning. In the script Claude returns, each line already carries a cue; your job is to sanity-check that the cues match the words and fix any that feel off.

What a well-directed script looks like
  • The description names a speaker, a pace and a warmth, not ten adjectives.
  • Expressions vary across the script, following the story's ups and downs.
  • Lines are short enough to say in one breath, because that is how they will be read.

Choosing a voice

Inside the TTS tool you also pick a named voice before you press run. Tools offer many options that differ by language, by apparent gender, by pitch and by intended use, and the creator picked a storyteller-style voice for the kids' explainer. The voice you choose and the description you write work together: a storyteller voice plus a gentle description reinforce each other, while a crisp news voice would pull against a bedtime-story description. Audition two or three before committing, because the difference is obvious once you hear your own lines.

Directing a natural read

The difference between a flat read and a natural one is mostly in the writing, not the tool. Three habits carry most of the weight. Write the way people speak, with short sentences and plain words. Put one idea per line, so the pauses fall in sensible places. And let the emotion move: a read that is curious, then amazed, then calm feels alive, while one steady tone feels mechanical. If a line still reads oddly, change the words before you blame the voice; TTS reads punctuation and phrasing closely, so a comma or a shorter sentence often fixes it.

Common mistakes with TTS
  • Very long sentences, which the voice reads in one rushed breath.
  • Numbers and abbreviations left raw, which may be read in unexpected ways; spell them out when it matters.
  • Every line the same expression, which flattens the whole read.
  • Blaming the voice for a line that is simply hard to say; rewrite the line.

Recap
  • TTS generates a spoken recording from written text, and modern TTS is controllable.
  • Two separate controls: a voice description for the whole narration, and an expression on each line.
  • Write descriptions with a few concrete qualities; vary expressions to follow the story.
  • Choose a named voice that agrees with your description and your audience, and audition a few.
  • A natural read comes mostly from speech-shaped writing; fix an odd line by rewriting it.
  • Our coded examples use a local Windows SAPI stand-in voice; the real workflow uses Google AI Studio.
