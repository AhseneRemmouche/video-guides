Motion-graphics design principles

Timing, easing, hierarchy, colour and restraint - what makes an explainer feel clear instead of busy.

A good explainer does not just move; it moves in a way that helps you understand. The difference between a clear animation and a busy one is rarely the subject matter, it is a handful of design choices about timing, emphasis and restraint. This chapter is the background reading behind the 2D explainer in step 9: what makes motion graphics read as calm and clear instead of frantic, and why the small choices in our own code were made the way they were.

Timing is the first design decision

In an explainer, the voice-over sets the clock and the picture follows. Our airplane video is built from nine narration lines, and each line owns a slice of the timeline: a scene begins when its line begins and ends when the line ends. That is why the animation never races ahead of the narrator or lags behind. Good timing means a viewer's eye arrives at the thing being described exactly as they hear it described.

Hold long enough to read

A common beginner error is cutting too fast. If a label appears and vanishes before a viewer can read it, the information is lost even though it was technically shown. A useful rule of thumb: anything the viewer must read needs to stay on screen long enough to be read twice at a calm pace. Because our scenes are tied to whole spoken lines, they naturally hold for a comfortable beat.

Easing: why nothing should move at a constant speed

Real objects accelerate and decelerate; they do not snap instantly to full speed. Animation feels natural when movement eases in and out rather than running linearly. This is what easing means, and it works hand in hand with interpolation, the act of computing the in-between values along a path. Our 2D scenes use a classic smoothstep curve so motion starts gently, speeds up, and settles softly.

// smoothstep: slow at the ends, quick in the middle
const ease = (p) => (p <= 0 ? 0 : p >= 1 ? 1 : p * p * (3 - 2 * p));
New to easing and interpolation? A plain explanation

Interpolation answers the question "given a start value, an end value and how far along I am, what is the value right now?" If you just blend them evenly you get linear motion. Easing bends that blend so the fraction grows slowly at first, faster in the middle, then slowly again. The same idea powers movement, fades and scale changes. You will meet it again as Remotion's interpolate helper in the Remotion chapter.

Visual hierarchy: guide the eye

Every frame should answer one question instantly: where do I look first? That is visual hierarchy. You create it with size, position, contrast and motion. The most important thing is the biggest, or the brightest, or the only thing moving. When everything shouts at once, nothing is heard. In our explainer, each scene has a single focus, a plane, a word, an arrow of lift, and the rest of the frame stays quiet to support it.

Colour and contrast

Colour in an explainer has a job, not just a look. Use a small, consistent palette: a calm background, one or two accent colours for the things that matter, and reserve a strong colour for the single idea you most want remembered. In our airplane scenes, the lift arrows are a distinct colour from the thrust arrows precisely so the viewer can tell the two forces apart at a glance. Contrast is what makes text and key shapes readable; light text on a busy mid-tone disappears, so keep important elements well separated from their background in brightness.

A workable colour approach
  • One background family, kept quiet.
  • One or two accent colours used consistently to mean the same thing each time.
  • Strong contrast between text and whatever sits behind it.

Restraint: the hardest and most important skill

Most amateur motion graphics fail by doing too much: everything spins, bounces, pulses and slides at once. Restraint is choosing not to. Animate a property only when the movement carries meaning, such as a plane rising to show lift, and leave the rest still. Decoration that does not explain anything competes with the thing that does. The creator even noted that the background music an early run produced was a bit annoying; that is the same principle in audio form, an extra element earning its place or being cut.

Signs an explainer is too busy
  • Text disappears before you can finish reading it.
  • Several things move at once and you do not know where to look.
  • Colours change meaning from scene to scene.
  • Effects and music that do not help you understand anything.
Recap
  • Timing comes first: pin each scene to its narration line and hold long enough to read.
  • Ease movement with a smoothstep-style curve so it feels natural, not mechanical.
  • Build visual hierarchy so the eye knows where to look; one focus per frame.
  • Use a small, consistent palette and strong contrast; give each colour a job.
  • Practise restraint: animate only what explains something, and cut the rest.
