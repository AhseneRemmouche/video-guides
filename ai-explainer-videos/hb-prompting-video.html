Prompting Claude for video

Writing the script prompt and the animation prompt so you get usable output, and how to iterate.

The whole AI workflow in this guide rests on two pieces of writing: a prompt that asks Claude for a voice-over script, and a second prompt that asks it to turn the finished voice-over into an animated video. Neither prompt is magic wording. They are plain requests that tell the model exactly what you want, in what form, and what it must not do. This chapter is the background reading for step 3 and step 5: what the two prompts are really saying, how to fill their blanks, how to iterate, and why the same prompt can give you a different result every time.

Two prompts, one pipeline

It helps to see the two prompts as the two ends of a single pipeline. The first prompt produces words for a voice to read. Those words go to a text-to-speech tool, which turns them into an audio file. The audio then comes back to Claude with the second prompt, which asks for a motion-graphics explainer video timed to that audio. Nothing in the middle is automatic: you carry each result by hand to the next stage.

What Prompt 1 actually asks for

The first prompt does more than say "write a script". It tells Claude the script is for a TTS tool, that the tool accepts a description of the voice and a separate note for each line, and that the finished audio will come back to make a motion-graphics explainer. In other words, it tells the model the whole downstream plan. That context is why Claude returns a script shaped for speech: short, spoken-sounding lines, each carrying an expression cue such as curious or excited, plus a voice description for the narrator as a whole. Had the prompt only asked for "a script", you would likely get prose with stage directions the TTS tool cannot use.

New to prompts? What they are and why wording matters

A prompt is simply the text you send to a large language model. The model reads it and predicts a helpful continuation. It has no memory of your intentions beyond what you type, so anything you leave unsaid, it has to guess. Naming the downstream tool, the audience, the format and the constraints removes guesses and makes the output far more usable. Think of it as briefing a capable new colleague who will take you completely literally.

What Prompt 2 actually asks for

The second prompt attaches the voice-over and asks for a motion-graphics video whose timing matches the narration, and it explicitly says to produce no captions or subtitles. The timing instruction matters because the audio is the backbone: every scene should land on the sentence it illustrates. The no-captions instruction matters because burned-in text is hard to remove later and clutters a clean animation. Saying what you do not want is as useful as saying what you do.

Filling the placeholders

Prompt 1 has two blanks, written as [audience] and [topic]. You replace them with your own words before sending. The example in the video is an audience of a young child and a topic of how airplanes fly, but the blanks are the whole point: they are the dials you turn to make a different video without rewriting anything else.

A detail worth keeping: changing [audience] changes far more than one word. A script for a seven year old uses simple images and a warm, playful read; a script for adults can carry more detail and a steadier tone. The creator made both a children's and an adult version of the same airplane topic, and they feel like different videos. Changing [topic] swaps the subject wholesale. Treat the two blanks as the only things you need to edit for a brand-new explainer.

What good filling looks like
  • Be concrete: "a 7 year old child" guides the read better than "kids".
  • Keep the topic to one clear idea per video, not three.
  • You can write the prompt in English or Arabic; English tends to be quicker to type and edit.

Iterating: reading, judging, re-running

You will rarely accept the very first script or the very first video. Iteration is the normal rhythm of this workflow, and it is cheap at the script stage and slower at the video stage, so most of your judgement happens on the words.

Iterating on the script

Read Claude's script out loud. If a line is a tongue-twister, too long for one breath, or sounds like writing rather than speech, say so and ask for that line to be rewritten. You can also ask to change the expression cues ("make line three sound more surprised") or the overall voice description ("warmer, slower, like a bedtime story"). Because this stage is just text, you can go back and forth several times in a minute.

Iterating on the video

The video stage is where patience pays. On the creator's machine, a run with Opus at medium effort took roughly ten to twenty minutes. So before you re-run, be specific about what to fix: "the second scene is too fast", "drop the background music", "make the plane enter from the left". A precise note turns one slow run into a real improvement instead of a lottery ticket. The model works in Co-work mode, summarises what it did, and gives you a file to download; step 6 covers that loop in detail.

Common mistakes when iterating
  • Re-running with no feedback and hoping it comes out better. Say what was wrong.
  • Changing five things at once, so you cannot tell which change helped.
  • Forgetting to re-attach the voice-over if you start a fresh chat for Prompt 2.

Why the same prompt gives different results

Large language models are non-deterministic: the same prompt can produce a different answer each time it runs. This is not a bug, and it is not the model being unreliable. It is how these systems sample their output. For you it has two consequences. First, if a run disappoints, running again can genuinely help, because you get a fresh attempt. Second, you cannot promise an identical video twice from the AI workflow alone, which is one reason Parts 2 and 3 of this guide rebuild the same kind of video in code, where the output is exactly reproducible.

New to non-determinism? A quick mental model

At each step the model holds a set of plausible next words with different likelihoods, and it picks among them with an element of chance rather than always taking the single most likely one. That small randomness, repeated across a long answer, is enough to send two runs down noticeably different paths. Settings such as the model and its effort level influence quality and speed, but they do not make the output perfectly repeatable. When you need byte-for-byte repeatability, you reach for code, not a prompt.

A note on the model and mode

The prompts are only half of the setup; where you type them matters too. The video uses Claude in Co-work mode rather than ordinary chat, with Opus at medium effort because it was fast enough and good enough, and it mentions that a newly released Sonnet model might also do the job. You do not need to memorise these choices, but you should know that the same two prompts behave differently depending on the model, the effort and the mode, all of which step 2 walks through. The same prompting instincts carry over when you later ask Claude for a 3D scene in step 14.

Recap
  • The workflow is a two-prompt pipeline: a script prompt, then a video prompt, with a voice-over made in between.
  • Prompt 1 tells Claude the downstream plan, so it returns a speech-shaped script with per-line expressions and a voice description.
  • Prompt 2 attaches the audio and asks for timing-matched motion graphics with no captions.
  • Fill [audience] and [topic] to make a new video; the audience changes the whole feel, not just a word.
  • Iterate cheaply on the script and deliberately on the slow video runs, giving specific feedback each time.
  • Non-determinism means re-running can help, but also that only code gives you an exactly reproducible result.
