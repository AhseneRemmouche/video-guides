Publishing and the ethics of AI video

Exporting, aspect ratios and captions, plus labelling AI-generated content and respecting copyright and voices.

Making the video is one job; putting it in front of people responsibly is another. This chapter covers the practical side of finishing, exporting the file, choosing an aspect ratio for the platform, and adding captions for accessibility, and then the ethical side: labelling AI-generated content clearly, and respecting copyright, people's likenesses and their voices. It is background reading around step 7. This is general education, not legal advice, and the rules differ from place to place, so treat it as a way to think rather than a rulebook.

Exporting a finished file

However you built the video, you end up with a single file to share. From the AI workflow, Claude gives you a downloadable video in Co-work mode. From Remotion, the render command writes the file; our own renders produced an MP4 using the H.264 video codec and AAC audio, which is the most widely compatible combination for the web and social platforms. If a destination needs something different, you re-export rather than editing the file in place.

New to codecs and containers? The short version

A video file has two parts worth knowing. The container, such as MP4, is the box that holds the streams. The codec is how the picture and sound inside are compressed, such as H.264 for video and AAC for audio. MP4 with H.264 and AAC plays almost everywhere, which is why it is the safe default. A different container or codec can be smaller or higher quality but may not play on every device.

Aspect ratio and platforms

The shape of the frame should match where the video will live. Our explainer is 1280 by 720, a 16:9 widescreen frame that suits a computer, a landscape web player or a large screen. Phone-first feeds usually want a tall 9:16 frame; square 1:1 frames sit well in mixed feeds. The shape is not a last-minute setting: it changes how much room your layout has and where the eye falls, so decide it before you design the scenes. In Remotion it is simply the composition's width and height, as covered in the Remotion chapter.

Related to shape is resolution, the pixel count. More pixels means a sharper image and a larger file. For most explainers 720p or 1080p is plenty; going higher mainly grows the file. Match the resolution to how the video will be watched rather than chasing the biggest number.

Captions and accessibility

An explainer should work for people who cannot hear it, cannot see it well, or are watching with the sound off, which on social feeds is most people. The single most valuable addition is captions. Note the useful distinction: our second prompt deliberately asks for no burned-in captions, because text baked into the picture cannot be turned off or translated. The better path is a separate caption track or subtitle file that players can show, hide and translate. Beyond captions, a few habits widen your audience: strong contrast so text is legible, type large enough to read on a phone, and not relying on colour alone to carry meaning.

Labelling AI-generated content

If AI made or substantially shaped the video, say so. Being open about it builds trust and is increasingly expected by platforms and, in some places, required. A short, honest note, that the script, voice or animation was AI-generated, is enough; you do not need to apologise for it. Several platforms also offer a built-in toggle to mark content as AI-made, and using it is good practice. The aim is simple: a viewer should never be misled about whether what they are watching is real footage or a generated piece.

Honest labelling in practice
  • State plainly that the video is AI-generated where viewers will see it.
  • Use a platform's AI-content toggle when it offers one.
  • Do not present a generated voice or scene as a real recording of a real event.

Copyright, likeness and voices

Generating content does not switch off other people's rights. Three areas deserve care.

Copyright

Do not feed in or reproduce material you do not have the right to use: music, footage, images, scripts or characters that belong to someone else. The background music an AI run adds is a good example to check, since you are responsible for what you publish even if a tool generated it. When you need music or images, use material that is clearly licensed for your use.

Likeness

A person's face and image carry rights in many places. Generating a video that depicts a real, identifiable person, especially doing or saying things they did not, can cause real harm and can be unlawful. Avoid recreating real individuals without their clear permission.

Voices

The same caution applies to voices. A generated voice that imitates a specific real person is a likeness of a kind and should not be used to impersonate them. Choosing a neutral, generic narrator, as our examples do, sidesteps the issue entirely.

Laws vary, and this is not legal advice Rules on AI disclosure, copyright, likeness and voice differ by country and change over time. This chapter is general guidance to help you act responsibly. For anything with real stakes, check the current rules for your audience and platform, and seek proper advice.

A short pre-publish checklist

Before a video goes out, a quick pass catches most problems: the aspect ratio and resolution fit the platform; the file plays as MP4 with H.264 and AAC; captions or a transcript are available; the AI origin is labelled; and nothing in the video uses someone else's work, face or voice without permission. None of this takes long, and it is far easier than taking a published video down.

Recap
  • Export to a widely compatible MP4 (H.264 video, AAC audio); re-export rather than edit in place.
  • Choose the aspect ratio for the platform first; 16:9 for landscape, 9:16 for phones, 1:1 for mixed feeds.
  • Prefer a separate caption track over burned-in text, and design so the video works with the sound off.
  • Label AI-generated content openly and use platform AI toggles where offered.
  • Respect copyright, likeness and voices; a generic narrator avoids impersonation.
  • Laws vary and change; this is general guidance, not legal advice.
