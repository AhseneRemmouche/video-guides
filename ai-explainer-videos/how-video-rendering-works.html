How a video is actually made: frames, fps and audio

The ideas every approach shares - frames per second, a timeline, syncing audio, and what rendering to a codec means.

In Part 1 you made a video without knowing how a video actually works — and that is fine, because the AI did the hard part. But Parts 2 and 3 ask you to build one in code, and for that you need a clear mental model of what a video is. This page gives you that model in plain language. Read it once and the later steps will feel like filling in details you already understand.

There is no code here and nothing to install. Just five ideas: frames, frames per second, a timeline, how motion is faked between moments, and what it means to turn all of that into a single video file.

A video is a stack of still pictures

A video is not really “moving”. It is a long series of still images, called frames, shown one after another so quickly that your eye reads them as motion. A flip-book is the honest version of this: each page is slightly different, and flicking through fast enough looks like movement. Every video you have ever watched is a very fast, very neat flip-book.

Frames per second sets the smoothness

How fast is “fast enough”? That is frames per second, written fps: the number of still frames shown in each second of video. More frames per second means smoother motion and more pictures to prepare. Common values are 24, 30 and 60. The coded examples in this guide use 30 fps, a comfortable middle choice.

Fps also connects time to counting. If a video runs at 30 fps, then 1 second is 30 frames, and a clip that lasts about 37 seconds is roughly 37 × 30 frames long. In this guide's example the voice-over lasts 37.295 seconds, which at 30 fps works out to 1119 frames — a number you will meet again as the video's length in frames.

How big is each frame? Resolution and shape

Each frame is a grid of coloured dots (pixels). Resolution is how many dots across and down, for example 1920 by 1080. The aspect ratio is the shape that grid makes: 16:9 is the familiar wide rectangle, 9:16 is the tall shape for phone-first video. You pick these once for the whole video; every frame shares the same size and shape.

A timeline pins events to moments in time

If a video is frames laid out in time, then a timeline is the ruler that says when things happen. For an explainer, the most important things on the timeline are the narration cues: the moment each spoken line starts and ends. Line those up with the animation and the pictures land on the words — which is exactly what Prompt 2 asked the AI to do in Part 1, and exactly what you will arrange by hand in the code parts.

Motion between moments: interpolation and easing

You do not hand-draw all 1119 frames. Instead you describe a few key moments — a dot starts on the left at second 0 and is on the right at second 2 — and let the computer fill in the frames between. Filling in the in-between values is called interpolation. Done in a straight, even line it looks mechanical; real motion speeds up and slows down. Shaping that speed so things start gently and settle softly is called easing, and it is what makes animation feel natural instead of robotic.

The key idea for the code parts: a frame is a function of time

Here is the single thought that makes the coded approach click. Instead of storing a drawing for every frame, you write one recipe that takes a time (or a frame number) and returns the picture for that instant: give it second 0 and it draws the opening; give it second 2 and it draws the dot on the right. The computer just calls that recipe once per frame. Because the same recipe answers for any time, the preview you watch and the final rendered file are guaranteed to match — they came from the same function. This is the heart of how the Remotion examples in Part 2 work, and the audio cues keep the pictures and the voice in step, something called audio sync.

Rendering: turning frames into one file

So far we have frames, a rate, a timeline and recipes for motion. Putting them together into a single file you can share is called rendering. The computer draws every frame in order, pairs them with the audio, and packs the result into one file. To keep that file small enough to store and send, the pictures and sound are compressed using a codec — a scheme for squeezing video and audio without obviously hurting quality. The familiar MP4 is the container that holds the compressed video and audio together.

Why “render” takes time — even on a fast computer

Rendering is not instant because the computer really does produce every single frame, one by one, then compress them. A 37-second video at 30 fps is over a thousand drawings to make and pack. This is also why the AI workflow in Part 1 took roughly 10–20 minutes: there is genuine work behind the scenes, whoever or whatever is doing it. The coded approach in Part 2 does this on your own machine, so you can watch it happen and re-run it whenever you like.

The one picture to keep. A video is still pictures (frames) shown many times per second (fps), arranged on a timeline; motion between key moments is filled in by interpolation and shaped by easing; and rendering draws every frame, syncs the audio, and compresses it with a codec into one MP4. Every part of this guide is some version of that same idea.
Recap
  • A video is a fast stack of still frames; fps (here 30) sets how many play each second and links time to frame count.
  • A timeline pins events — especially narration cues — to moments in time.
  • Interpolation fills the frames between key moments; easing makes that motion feel natural.
  • The core coding idea: a frame is a function of time, so preview and final render always match.
  • Rendering draws every frame, syncs the audio, and compresses it with a codec into one MP4.
