The idea: a voice-over plus one prompt becomes an explainer video

What this workflow produces, and the two-prompt pipeline that makes an animated explainer with AI.

▶ Watch this part (00:00:00)

This guide starts with a simple, almost surprising idea from Codezilla's video: you can make a finished animated explainer video with no video editor and no code at all. You write two instructions, let an AI do the heavy lifting in between, and you get a video you can download.

In this first step we look at the whole idea from a distance (at 0:00). You will not do anything on your computer yet. The goal is to see how the pieces fit together, so that every later step makes sense. Think of it as reading the map before the journey.

The big idea: two prompts and one AI

The creator's trick is that the work is split into two halves, and each half is kicked off by one written instruction, called a prompt. You give both prompts to Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant.

  • Prompt 1 asks for the words. Claude writes a short narration script - the voice-over - for whatever topic and audience you choose.
  • In the middle, a different tool speaks the words. You take that script to Google AI Studio, whose text-to-speech tool (powered by Gemini) reads it aloud and gives you an audio file.
  • Prompt 2 asks for the picture. You bring that audio back to Claude and ask it to build an animated motion-graphics video that matches the narration. Claude produces a video you can download.

That is the entire no-code workflow: write, voice, animate. Two prompts, one AI assistant, and one short visit to a second website in the middle.

Why the voice comes first

It feels backwards to record the narration before there is any video. But it is the key to the whole method. Once the spoken audio exists, its length and timing are fixed: you know exactly when each sentence is said. Claude can then line the animation up to that timing, so a shape appears right as the narrator mentions it. If you made the animation first, you would have to force the voice to fit it, which is much harder.

New to the word "prompt"? What it is and why it matters

A prompt is simply the message you type to an AI to tell it what you want. There is nothing technical about it - it is written in ordinary language. A good prompt is specific: it names the task, the audience and any rules to follow. This workflow leans entirely on two carefully written prompts, so we show both of them word for word in later steps.

New to "motion graphics" and "explainer video"? What they are

An explainer video is a short video whose only job is to make one idea clear, usually with a friendly narrator and simple animation. Motion graphics means animated graphic design - moving shapes, text and illustrations - rather than filmed footage of real people. Most explainer videos you see online are motion graphics. That is exactly what this workflow produces.

The example that makes it click: how airplanes fly

The video uses one running example, and we keep it through the whole guide: a short explainer about how airplanes fly. The creator points out something the method makes easy (around the middle of the video): because you choose the audience in the first prompt, the very same topic can become two completely different videos. He made one for a seven-year-old child and one for adults, and they look and sound very different even though the subject is identical.

Why airplanes? Because it is a clear, visual idea - air moving over a wing - that an animation can show far better than words alone. The creator's version did well online, which is part of why the method is worth learning.

What this guide covers: three parts

The video shows one way to make the video (the AI workflow above). You also asked for two code-based ways to make the same kind of video, so this guide has three parts. They are independent - you can read Part 1 and stop, or jump straight to the code.

  1. Part 1 - the AI workflow (Steps 01 to 07). The no-code method from the video: set up Claude, write the script, voice it in Google AI Studio, and let Claude animate it.
  2. Part 2 - build it with Remotion (Steps 08 to 11). Make the video yourself in code, using Remotion, which renders React components into a video file.
  3. Part 3 - go 3D with Three.js (Steps 12 to 15). Add real 3D with Three.js, on its own and inside Remotion.
An honest note about how the video gets made. The video shows you the two prompts and the download, but it does not reveal how Claude internally turns the audio into a finished video file. We will not pretend to know a mechanism the video does not show. In Part 1 we simply describe what you see: Claude, working in a special mode, produces a downloadable video. Parts 2 and 3 then show one concrete, repeatable way to build the same kind of video in code.
What you should see by the end of Part 1. A short animated explainer video, with narration, that you downloaded from Claude - made from nothing but two typed prompts and one trip to Google AI Studio. No video editor, no timeline software, no code.
Common early misunderstandings. This is not one magic button - there are two separate prompts with a voice-over step in between, and the order matters. It is also not fully repeatable: AI output varies from run to run (more on that later), so do not expect an identical result each time. And the video is not filmed footage; it is animated motion graphics.
Recap
  • The workflow is write, voice, animate: Prompt 1 makes a script, Google AI Studio voices it, Prompt 2 makes the video.
  • The voice-over is made first so the animation can be timed to match it.
  • The running example is "how airplanes fly"; the audience you choose changes the whole video.
  • The guide has three parts: the AI workflow (Part 1), Remotion (Part 2), and Three.js (Part 3).
  • The video does not show how Claude builds the video internally, so we do not invent that; Parts 2 and 3 show a real coded way.
