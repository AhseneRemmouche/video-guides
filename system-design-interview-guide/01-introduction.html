Introduction: What the Interview Tests

What a system-design interview really measures — gluing a system together, not writing code — and what this crash course covers.

▶ Watch this part (00:00)

Welcome to a crash course on the ideas a system design interview expects you to know. This first page sets the scene: what the interview is really testing, and a map of the topics the rest of the guide walks through. You do not need to memorise anything yet — just get a feel for the shape of the subject.

What this course covers

The course is a broad, beginner-friendly tour of how large applications are built and kept running. It covers scalability, reliability, data handling, and high-level architecture, explained with plain-English examples and lots of diagrams (at 0:00). It is deliberately wide rather than deep: the goal is to recognise every concept an interviewer might raise, and to be able to talk about each one with confidence.

The interview is not a coding test

Here is the single most important thing to understand before you walk in: a system-design interview has little to do with writing code (at 0:23). The interviewer does not want to watch you type out a function or solve a puzzle. They want to see how you glue a whole system together — how the pieces connect, where the data flows, and which trade-offs you are willing to accept.

In a coding interview there is usually one correct answer. In a system-design interview there are many reasonable answers, and the interesting part is the reasoning behind yours. Saying “I would use a cache here, and here is the cost of doing that” is worth far more than naming a fashionable tool.

Why we start with a single computer

Before designing large-scale distributed systems — systems spread across many machines — it helps to understand the high-level architecture of just one computer (at 0:45). If you know how a single machine stores and processes data, the bigger picture (many machines working together) is much easier to reason about. That is exactly where the next page goes.

New to the term “system design”? What it is and why it matters

System design is the practice of deciding how the parts of a software system fit together: which servers do what, where data lives, how requests travel, and how the whole thing stays fast and reliable as it grows. It is the “big picture” work that happens before and around the code. Interviewers test it because real engineering work is mostly about these decisions, not about clever one-off functions.

What does “distributed system” mean?

A distributed system is one that runs on more than one computer at the same time, with those computers cooperating over a network to look like a single service to the user. Most large websites are distributed systems. They bring big advantages (handling more users, surviving a machine failure) and new problems (keeping data in sync, dealing with slow or broken network links) — many later pages exist to tackle exactly those problems.

What is a “trade-off”?

A trade-off is a choice where getting more of one good thing means getting less of another. In system design there is rarely a free win: making reads faster can make writes slower, adding a backup server costs money, and so on. Good answers do not pretend a choice is perfect — they name what it gives up. Being comfortable with trade-offs is the main skill this course builds.

In an interview, say…

“Before I draw anything, let me clarify the requirements and the scale we are designing for. Then I will sketch the main components and how data moves between them, and for each important choice I will call out the trade-off I am accepting.” This shows you lead with reasoning, not with a favourite tool.

Common mistake

Jumping straight into code, or naming a specific database or framework in the first thirty seconds. The interviewer is testing how you think about the whole system and its trade-offs. Start with the problem and the connections; pick concrete technologies later, and explain why.

Recap
  • A system-design interview tests how you connect a whole system and reason about trade-offs — not whether you can write code.
  • There is usually more than one good answer; your explanation matters more than the choice itself.
  • This course is a broad tour: computer and app internals, design principles, reliability and speed, networking and APIs, then data and scaling.
  • We begin with a single computer because understanding one machine makes distributed systems far easier to grasp.
