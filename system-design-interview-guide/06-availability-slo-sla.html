Availability, SLOs & SLAs

Measuring uptime in nines, the downtime each nine allows, and the difference between SLOs, SLAs, reliability and redundancy.

▶ Watch this part (10:40)

Once your system is spread across machines, the next question is simple to ask and hard to answer: is it up when people need it? This page covers availability — how we measure it in "nines", the difference between the goals you set yourself (SLOs) and the promises you make to customers (SLAs), and the techniques that keep a system standing when parts of it fail.

Availability, measured in nines

Availability is a measure of a system's operational performance — really, it is asking "is the system up and running when users need it?" (at 10:40). We usually express it as a percentage of time the system is working, and in this world people chase extra nines. The presenter calls out the "golden five nines" as the gold standard (at 11:02).

The percentages look almost identical on paper, but the downtime they allow is wildly different. A service at 99.9% can be down for about 8.76 hours a year; add two more nines to reach 99.999% and you are down for only about 5 minutes a year (at 11:02). We talk about the flip side, uptime, in the same breath.

New to "nines"? How the percentages map to downtime

A year has about 525,600 minutes. "99.9% available" means the system may be down for 0.1% of those minutes — roughly 526 minutes, or 8.76 hours. Each extra nine divides the allowed downtime by ten: 99.99% allows about 53 minutes, 99.999% about 5 minutes. That is why teams obsess over a single decimal place: one more nine is a tenfold promise.

SLO vs SLA: goals versus promises

Two acronyms come up constantly here, and interviewers love to see you keep them straight. A service level objective (SLO) is an internal target you set for your own system's performance and availability (at 11:27). The presenter's example: "our web service should respond to requests within 300 milliseconds, 99.9% of the time" (at 11:50). It is a goal you hold yourself to.

A service level agreement (SLA) is a formal contract with your users or customers — the minimum level of service you commit to provide (at 11:50). If your SLA guarantees 99.99% availability and you drop below it, you may owe refunds or other compensation (at 12:12). An SLO is a promise to yourself; an SLA is a promise to someone else, with consequences attached.

SLO vs SLA — a one-line way to remember it

O for Objective, O for "our own goal." An SLO lives inside the team; missing it triggers an internal fix, not a payout. An SLA is a signed agreement with customers; missing it can cost real money. Teams deliberately set the SLO tighter than the SLA so that an internal alarm goes off well before the external promise is at risk.

Building resilience: expect the unexpected

Hitting those numbers means designing for failure, not just for the happy path. The presenter describes two main tactics (at 12:12). The first is redundancy: keep a backup component ready so that if one part fails, another immediately takes its place. The second is graceful degradation: design the system so that if some features go down, the core functionality still works.

How we measure resilience

The video names three related measures for this whole aspect of a system (at 12:34): reliability means the system works correctly and consistently; fault tolerance is how well it handles unexpected failures or attacks; and redundancy, as above, is about having backups so that if one part fails another is ready to replace it.

In an interview, say this

"I'd set an SLO stricter than our SLA — say target 99.99% internally while contracting 99.9% with customers — so an internal alert fires before we ever breach the agreement. To hit it I'd add redundancy with automatic failover and design the non-core features to degrade gracefully." That one sentence connects the numbers to concrete engineering.

Common mistake

Using SLO and SLA as if they were the same thing, or promising "100% uptime." No real system is up 100% of the time — that is why we measure in nines and plan for downtime. Treat the SLA as a floor with penalties, keep the SLO above it, and never claim a number you cannot defend with redundancy and graceful degradation.

Recap
  • Availability is measured in nines; 99.9% allows about 8.76 hours of downtime a year, 99.999% only about 5 minutes.
  • Each extra nine cuts allowed downtime roughly tenfold, so small percentage gains are a big deal.
  • An SLO is an internal goal (e.g. respond within 300 ms, 99.9% of the time); an SLA is a customer contract (e.g. 99.99% uptime) whose breach can mean refunds.
  • Build resilience with redundancy (a backup that fails over) and graceful degradation (core keeps working when extras fail).
  • We judge all of this with reliability, fault tolerance and redundancy — and never promise 100%.
