Glossary

Every technical term in this guide, explained for beginners.

This page explains every technical word used in the guide, in plain language. Terms are grouped by topic, so you can also read a whole group to get the big picture. Each entry ends with the place in this guide where you will meet the idea in practice: a lab (where you run code from the trailhead-ai project) or a concept chapter (where the ideas are explained and paired with a chapter of the book).

The big picture

AI engineering
The work of building useful applications on top of models that someone else has already trained. Instead of training a model from scratch, you choose a model, write prompts, connect it to your data and tools, and above all measure whether the result is good enough. In this guide: What is AI engineering and lab 01.
Foundation model
A large model trained on a huge amount of general data so that it can be adapted to many different tasks. Text models, image models and models that handle several kinds of input all belong to this family. You use it as a building block rather than building your own. In this guide: Foundation models.
LLM (large language model)
A foundation model that works with text: it reads text and predicts what text should come next, one token at a time. Chatbots, code assistants and our bike-shop support bot are all built on LLMs. Our app model is llama3.2 and our judge model is gemma4, both running locally. In this guide: Foundation models and lab 01.
Transformer
The neural network design used by almost all modern LLMs. Its key ingredient is attention, which lets every token look at the other tokens in the input. You do not need to know its internals to build applications, but it explains things like the context window and the KV cache. In this guide: Foundation models.
Attention
The mechanism inside a transformer that decides, for each token, how much every other token should matter. It is how the model connects "it" to the right noun earlier in a sentence. Its cost grows with the length of the input, which is one reason long prompts are slower. In this guide: Foundation models.
Parameters (weights)
The numbers learned during training that make up a model. "3B" in a model description means about three billion parameters; llama3.2 is a 3B model and its download is about 2.0 GB. More parameters usually means a more capable but slower and more memory-hungry model. In this guide: Foundation models and lab 01.

Models and tokens

Token
The small unit of text a model reads and writes: often a whole short word, sometimes a piece of a longer word, a space or a punctuation mark. Prices, speed limits and the context window are all measured in tokens, not words or characters. Our wrapper aie/llm.py prints the token counts of every call. In this guide: lab 01 and lab 02.
Tokenizer
The program that cuts text into tokens and turns them into numbers the model can process, and back again. Each model family has its own tokenizer, so the same sentence can be a different number of tokens for different models. In this guide: Foundation models.
Context window
The maximum number of tokens a model can handle in one call, counting both your input and its answer. Everything the model should "know" for this request (instructions, documents, chat history) must fit inside it. This limit is one reason RAG sends only the most relevant chunks. In this guide: Foundation models and lab 07.
Inference
Using a trained model to produce an output, as opposed to training it. Every time our bot answers a question, that is one inference call. Inference speed and cost are what you optimise once an app is in use. In this guide: Inference optimization and lab 14.
Sampling
How the model picks the next token. The model gives a probability to every possible next token; sampling draws one according to those probabilities, then repeats. Settings such as temperature and top-p change how adventurous the draw is, which is why the same prompt can give different answers. In this guide: lab 02.
Temperature
A sampling setting that makes the model's choices more predictable (low values) or more varied (high values). At 0 the model almost always picks its most likely token, which is good for support answers and tests. Higher values suit brainstorming but raise the risk of odd or wrong text. In this guide: lab 02.
Top-p (nucleus sampling)
A sampling setting that keeps only the most likely tokens whose probabilities add up to p (for example 0.9) and draws from those. It cuts off the long tail of unlikely tokens. It is often tuned together with temperature. In this guide: lab 02.
Seed
A number that fixes the random choices made during sampling, so a run can be repeated. With the same model, prompt, settings and seed you usually get the same output, but not every server guarantees it. In this guide: lab 02.
Hallucination
When a model states something false or made up with full confidence. In our labs the bot without documents invented a long frame warranty, US shipping prices and fake order ids. Giving the model real sources (grounding) and checking answers with eval sets are the main defences. In this guide: lab 04 and lab 03.
Reasoning model (thinking model)
A model that writes hidden "thinking" tokens before its final answer, which can improve hard answers but costs time. Our judge gemma4 is one; the setting AIE_REASONING_EFFORT=none (sent as reasoning_effort) turned the thinking off and made one answer go from about 95–110 s to about 13 s on our machine. In this guide: lab 01 and lab 05.

Calling a model

Prompt
The text you send to a model to tell it what to do: instructions, context and the user's question. Small wording changes can change the answer a lot, so in our project prompts live in versioned files in prompts/ and are tested like code. In this guide: Prompt engineering and lab 08.
System prompt
A special message, usually placed first, that sets the model's role and rules for the whole conversation (for example "You are the support assistant of Trailhead Bikes; answer only from the documents"). The user's messages come after it. Models tend to give it more weight, but it is not a security boundary. In this guide: lab 01 and lab 08.
Few-shot prompting
Putting a few worked examples (input and the answer you want) in the prompt so the model copies the pattern. "Zero-shot" means no examples. Examples are a quick way to fix format or tone, but they use up context and cost tokens on every call. In this guide: Prompt engineering and lab 08.
Structured output
Asking the model to answer in a fixed machine-readable shape, usually JSON, so your code can use the result directly. Our helper chat_json checks the reply against a schema and retries if it does not fit. Remember the lesson of lab 03: valid JSON is not the same as correct content. In this guide: lab 03.
JSON Schema
A standard way to describe what a JSON document must look like: which fields, which types, which values or patterns are allowed. It is sent to the model (or used to check its reply) so the output can be trusted by code. In our project it is generated from Pydantic classes. In this guide: lab 03.
Pydantic
A Python library where you describe data as a class with typed fields, and it checks (validates) incoming data against that description. We use it for model outputs such as the judge's verdict, and it can produce a JSON Schema for you. In this guide: lab 03 and lab 05.
API
An application programming interface: a defined way for one program to ask another for something. Here it means sending an HTTP request with your messages to a model server and getting the reply back as JSON. Our code never talks to the model directly, only through this interface. In this guide: lab 01.
OpenAI-compatible API
A server that accepts the same requests and returns the same response format as OpenAI's API, so the official openai Python package can talk to it. Ollama offers one at http://localhost:11434/v1, which is why the same code could use a hosted provider by changing a few settings in .env. In this guide: lab 01.

Tools you install

Ollama
A free program that downloads open models and runs them on your own computer, with an OpenAI-compatible API. You fetch a model with a command such as ollama pull llama3.2. Everything in this guide runs locally through it, so you need no paid account. In this guide: lab 01.
uv
A fast tool that manages Python versions, project dependencies and the virtual environment for you. Commands like uv add openai install a package and record it in pyproject.toml; uv run runs a command inside the project's environment. In this guide: lab 01.
Virtual environment (venv)
A private folder of Python packages that belongs to one project, so its versions do not clash with other projects on the same computer. uv creates and uses it automatically (in a .venv folder). In this guide: lab 01.
JSONL (JSON Lines)
A text file where each line is one complete JSON object. It is easy to append to, read line by line and compare between versions, which is why our eval sets (evals/qa.jsonl) and run results use it. In this guide: lab 04.
SQLite
A small database stored in a single file, built into Python (import sqlite3) with no server to install. Our project uses it to store traces and user feedback in runs/traces.db. In this guide: lab 12.
pytest
The most common Python testing tool: it finds functions named test_… and reports which pass or fail. Our offline tests check the plumbing (chunking, BM25, RRF, keyword checks, guardrails, order permissions, the exact cache) without calling a model; uv run pytest should report 8 passed. In this guide: lab 01.

Retrieval and RAG

Embedding
A list of numbers (a vector) that represents the meaning of a piece of text, produced by an embedding model. Texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions, even if they use different words. We use nomic-embed-text, which expects the prefixes search_document: and search_query: . In this guide: lab 06.
Vector
An ordered list of numbers, which you can picture as an arrow or a point in space. Embeddings are vectors with hundreds of numbers; the maths for comparing them is the same as for 2-D arrows. In this guide: lab 06.
Cosine similarity
A score for how closely two vectors point in the same direction: near 1 means very similar, near 0 means unrelated. It is the usual way to compare embeddings; we compute it with numpy. In this guide: lab 06.
Chunk
A small piece of a longer document, cut so it can be searched and sent to the model on its own. Our 8 policy documents become 23 chunks. Chunks that are too big waste context; chunks that are too small lose the meaning. In this guide: lab 06.
Chunk overlap
Repeating the end of one chunk at the start of the next, so a sentence or idea cut at the border still appears whole in at least one chunk. In our chunk_doc each chunk repeats the last paragraph of the previous one (overlap=1). In this guide: lab 06.
BM25
A classic keyword-search scoring method: a chunk scores higher when it contains the query's words, especially rare ones, with a correction for long chunks. It is fast and needs no model, but misses synonyms: in our lab it missed a question that said "deliver" while the document said "ship". In this guide: lab 06.
IDF (inverse document frequency)
A weight that is high for words appearing in few chunks and low for words appearing everywhere. It is why "warranty" counts more than "the" in BM25. In this guide: lab 06.
Retriever
The component that, given a question, returns the most relevant chunks. In our project BM25, EmbeddingIndex and Hybrid are three retrievers with the same search method, so they can be swapped and compared. In this guide: lab 06.
Hybrid search
Combining keyword search (like BM25) with embedding search, so each covers the other's blind spots. The two result lists are merged, in our case with RRF. In this guide: lab 06.
RRF (reciprocal rank fusion)
A simple way to merge several ranked lists: each item gets 1 / (c + rank) from every list it appears in, and the totals are sorted. It only uses positions, not raw scores, so lists with very different score scales can be combined. We use c = 60, the common default. In this guide: lab 06.
Reranking
A second, more careful pass that reorders the first few results of a retriever, often with a slower model that reads the question and each chunk together. It trades some speed for better ordering. Our labs do not include a reranker; it is the usual next step. In this guide: RAG and agents.
RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)
A pattern where you first retrieve relevant documents, then put them in the prompt so the model answers from them. It lets a model use your own, up-to-date information without retraining. Our aie/rag.py retrieves 3 chunks and asks the model to answer only from them. In this guide: lab 07 and RAG and agents.
Grounding
Making an answer rely on given sources rather than on what the model remembers. A grounded answer can be checked against those sources. Prompts that say "answer only from the documents, otherwise say you don't know" are a grounding technique. In this guide: lab 07.
Citation
A reference, inside the answer, to the source chunk it came from (in our project a chunk id in square brackets, such as [returns#0]). Citations let users and code check the answer, and an output check can reject a citation to a chunk that was never retrieved. In this guide: lab 07 and lab 10.

Evaluation

Eval set (evaluation set)
A fixed list of test questions with what a good answer must contain, used to measure an AI system the same way every time. Ours is evals/qa.jsonl. Without one you cannot tell whether a change made things better or worse. In this guide: lab 04 and Evaluating AI systems.
Baseline
The score of the simplest version of your system, recorded so later versions can be compared to it. Our baseline is the bot with no documents; its keyword pass rate was about 30 %. In this guide: lab 04.
Metric
A number that summarises how well a system does on something you care about: pass rate, hit rate, latency, cost. A metric is only useful if it matches what users actually need, so check a few examples by hand. In this guide: Evaluation methods.
Exact match (and keyword checks)
Checking an answer by comparing it to an expected string, or by looking for required keywords. It is cheap and repeatable but brittle: in lab 04 a check passed because "not" appeared inside "note", and failed a correct polite refusal. In this guide: lab 04.
AI judge (LLM-as-a-judge)
Using a model to grade another model's answers, guided by a rubric. It can judge meaning, not just keywords, but it makes its own mistakes, so you compare it with human labels first. Our gemma4 judge agreed with the human labels on 17 of 20 answers. In this guide: lab 05.
Rubric
The written grading rules given to a judge (human or AI): what counts as correct, what counts as a failure. Wording matters: in lab 05 the judge followed the rubric literally and failed answers that added small invented extras. In this guide: lab 05.
Human labels
Grades written by a person for a set of answers, used as the reference to check automatic graders. They are judgements too and can be debatable. Ours are in evals/judge_calibration.jsonl. In this guide: lab 05.
Position bias
A tendency of AI judges to prefer an option because of where it appears (for example the first of two answers), not because it is better. A common fix is to ask twice with the order swapped and keep only consistent verdicts. In this guide: Evaluation methods.
Hit rate (hit@k)
For retrieval: the share of questions for which the right document appears in the top k results. Hit@1 asks "was it first?", hit@3 asks "was it in the top three?". In this guide: lab 06.
MRR (mean reciprocal rank)
A retrieval score that rewards putting the right result near the top: a question scores 1 if the right document is first, 1/2 if second, 1/3 if third, and so on; MRR is the average. In this guide: lab 06.
Precision and recall
Two complementary measures. Precision: of the items you flagged or retrieved, how many were right. Recall: of all the right items, how many you found. Raising one often lowers the other, for example in a guardrail that blocks too much or too little. In this guide: Evaluating AI systems and lab 10.

Prompts and security

Prompt injection
An attack where someone writes instructions in their input ("ignore your rules and…") hoping the model will obey them instead of yours. Models cannot reliably tell instructions from data, so you add checks outside the model and never give the model powers it should not use. In this guide: lab 10.
Indirect prompt injection
A prompt injection hidden in content the system reads by itself, such as a web page, an email or a retrieved document, rather than typed by the user. It matters most for RAG and agents, which pull in outside text. In this guide: lab 10 and RAG and agents.
Jailbreak
A prompt designed to make a model ignore its safety rules or its intended role, for example through role-play or tricky phrasing. It is related to prompt injection, but the target is the model's built-in limits. In this guide: Prompt engineering.
Guardrail
A check placed before or after the model that blocks, rewrites or flags bad input or output. Our aie/guardrails.py masks PII, looks for injection phrases and checks that answers cite only retrieved chunks. Simple guardrails are cheap but miss things, so measure them too. In this guide: lab 10.
PII (personally identifiable information)
Data that can identify a person, such as a name, email address, phone number or card number. You should avoid sending it to models and logs when it is not needed; our input guardrail replaces it with placeholders. In this guide: lab 10.

Agents

Agent
A system where the model decides, step by step, which tools to call, sees their results and continues until it can answer. It is more flexible than a fixed pipeline, but each extra step can go wrong, so its path must be checked. Our run_agent can look up orders and search the documents. In this guide: lab 09.
Tool calling (function calling)
A model feature where, instead of plain text, the model returns a request to run a named function with arguments; your code runs it and sends back the result. The model never runs code itself. Permissions belong in the tool: our make_get_order refuses orders of other customers whatever the prompt says. In this guide: lab 09.
Trajectory
The full list of steps an agent took: which tools, with which arguments, in which order. Checking it catches answers that look fine but were reached the wrong way; in lab 09 it showed the agent never searched the return policy. In this guide: lab 09.
Planning
The part of an agent's work where it decides which steps to take before or while taking them. Small models often plan poorly, for example by skipping a needed lookup. Clear tool descriptions and step limits help. In this guide: RAG and agents and lab 09.

Production: speed, cost, monitoring

Cache
A store of earlier results so a repeated request can be answered without calling the model again. Our ExactCache only reuses an answer when the question text is identical after simple normalising. It saves time and money but can serve stale answers if documents change. In this guide: lab 11.
Semantic cache
A cache that reuses an answer when a new question is close in meaning to an old one, measured with embeddings and a similarity threshold (0.9 by default in our SemanticCache). It hits more often than an exact cache but can return the answer to a question that only looks similar. In this guide: lab 11.
Router
A step that sorts each request and sends it to the right handler: a cheap model, a bigger model, a fixed reply or a human. Our route function asks a model to sort the message into one of four structured labels: smalltalk, policy question, order question or out of scope. In this guide: lab 11.
Latency
How long a user waits for a response. For model calls it is usually split into TTFT and TPOT. It depends on model size, prompt length, hardware and whether the model is already loaded. In this guide: lab 14.
TTFT (time to first token)
The time between sending a request and receiving the first token of the answer. With streaming it is what the user feels as "how fast it starts". Long prompts and loading a model raise it. In this guide: lab 14.
TPOT (time per output token)
The average time to produce each token after the first one. Total time is roughly TTFT plus TPOT times the number of output tokens, so short answers are faster. In this guide: lab 14.
Throughput
How much work a system completes per unit of time, for example tokens per second or requests per minute across all users. Latency is about one user; throughput is about the whole server. In this guide: Inference optimization.
Streaming
Receiving the answer in small pieces as it is generated instead of waiting for the whole text. It does not make the model faster, but users see progress sooner. Lab 14 measures it with stream=True. In this guide: lab 14.
Batching
Processing several requests together in one pass through the model, which uses the hardware better and raises throughput, sometimes at the cost of a little latency per request. Model servers do this for you. In this guide: Inference optimization.
KV cache
Memory where a transformer keeps intermediate results for tokens it has already processed, so each new token does not recompute everything. It speeds up generation but uses memory that grows with the length of the text. In this guide: Inference optimization.
Quantization
Storing a model's parameters with fewer bits (for example 4 instead of 16), which makes it smaller and faster at a small cost in quality. It is how 3B models fit on small GPUs like ours; models downloaded through Ollama are usually already quantized. In this guide: Inference optimization.
Tracing
Recording what happened during each request: the input, the prompt version, retrieved chunks, model output, tokens and timing. Traces let you debug a bad answer later. Our aie/tracing.py writes them to a SQLite file. In this guide: lab 12.
Observability
Being able to understand what a running system is doing from the data it records: traces, metrics, logs and feedback. For AI apps it also means watching answer quality over time, not only errors and speed. In this guide: Architecture and feedback and lab 12.
User feedback
Signals from users about answers: thumbs up or down, comments, or behaviour such as asking again. Linked to a trace, it shows which answers failed and gives new cases for your eval set. In this guide: lab 12.

Training and data

Finetuning
Further training an existing model on your own examples so it adapts its behaviour, style or format. It changes the model's weights, unlike prompting or RAG. It needs good data and an eval set to prove it helped. In this guide: Finetuning and lab 13.
SFT (supervised finetuning)
Finetuning on pairs of an input and the ideal answer, so the model learns to reply that way. It is the most common first kind of finetuning; lab 13 builds a dataset in this shape. In this guide: Finetuning.
Preference finetuning
Finetuning with examples of which of two answers people prefer, so the model learns to produce the better kind. Methods such as RLHF and DPO belong here. In this guide: Finetuning.
PEFT (parameter-efficient finetuning)
A family of methods that train only a small number of extra or selected parameters instead of the whole model. It needs much less memory and produces small files you can swap in and out. LoRA is the best-known example. In this guide: Finetuning.
LoRA (low-rank adaptation)
A PEFT method that keeps the original weights frozen and trains small extra matrices added to some layers. The result is a light "adapter" that can be combined with the base model. In this guide: Finetuning.
Synthetic data
Training or test examples generated by a model instead of written by people. It is fast and cheap but can be wrong or repetitive, so it must be filtered: lab 13 keeps a question–answer pair only if every number in its answer also appears in the source document. In this guide: lab 13 and Dataset engineering.
Deduplication
Removing repeated or near-identical examples from a dataset. Duplicates waste training, skew evaluation and can leak test questions into training data. Lab 13 normalises the text before comparing. In this guide: lab 13 and Dataset engineering.
