What AI engineering is

How building on foundation models differs from classic machine learning, and the layers of an AI application. Read with book chapter 1.

Read with chapter 1 · Introduction to Building AI Applications with Foundation Models of the book. This page explains the ideas in its own words and links them to the labs.

A few years ago, adding "AI" to a product usually meant collecting a dataset, training your own model and keeping a team busy maintaining it. Today many teams start differently: they take a large, general model that somebody else trained, call it over the network like any other service, and spend their effort on everything around it. That "everything around it" is what people now call AI engineering.

On this page you will learn where these big models come from, what changes when you build on a model you did not train, which kinds of applications people build, how to decide whether a project is worth it, and how the work is split into layers. Every idea is tied back to the small support bot you build in the labs.

Why this matters for your project. In lab 01 you send one question to a model and get an answer in a few seconds. It looks finished. The remaining thirteen labs exist because a working call is the start of an AI application, not the end. This chapter explains why.

The project you build in the labs

All labs work on one project, trailhead-ai: a customer support assistant for Trailhead Bikes, a fictional online bike shop with stores in Lyon, Grenoble and Annecy. The shop's policies (returns, shipping, warranty, battery care, sizing, service plans, stores, orders) are eight short documents invented for the labs. Everything runs on your own computer, for free, with Ollama serving open models: llama3.2 answers customers, the larger gemma4 acts as a judge, and nomic-embed-text turns text into vectors for search.

Keep this bot in mind as you read. Each concept below has a concrete place in it.

From language models to foundation models

A language model is a program that has learned how likely each piece of text is to come next. Give it "The shop is closed on" and it can tell you that "Monday" or "Sunday" are likely continuations and "banana" is not. The pieces it works with are called tokens: whole words, parts of words or punctuation marks. Generating text is just repeating "pick a likely next token, add it, look again" many times.

Language models are old. Simple ones that counted which words follow which have been used for decades in spell checkers and phone keyboards. What changed is the scale and the method of training.

Self-supervision: the data labels itself

Classic machine learning needs labelled examples. To train a spam filter, people had to mark thousands of e-mails as "spam" or "not spam". Labelling is slow and expensive, so datasets stayed small.

Language modelling avoids this problem. Any sentence already contains its own answers: hide the next word, ask the model to guess it, then reveal it. The "label" is simply the text that really came next. This is called self-supervision. Because no human has to label anything, a model can learn from an enormous amount of text: web pages, books, code, forums, documentation.

Scale: from language model to large language model

With a nearly unlimited supply of training text, researchers could make models much bigger. A model's size is usually given as its number of parameters: the adjustable numbers inside it that training tunes. The llama3.2 model in the labs has about 3 billion of them, and it is considered small. When a model is big enough and trained on enough text that it can follow instructions, summarize, translate and write code without being trained separately for each task, people call it a large language model (LLM). There is no exact size where "large" starts; the word describes what the model can do more than a number.

Multimodality: from LLM to foundation model

The same recipe works for more than text. Models can be trained on images paired with captions, on audio, on video frames. A model that handles several of these kinds of data is called multimodal. The umbrella term foundation model covers all these large, general models, text-only or multimodal. "Foundation" means they are a base you build many different applications on, instead of one model per task.

New to "training" and "inference"? What they are and why they matter

Training is the expensive, one-time phase where a model's parameters are adjusted by showing it huge amounts of data. It needs many specialised chips (GPUs) for weeks or months. Inference is using the finished model: you send it input, it produces output. When you run a lab, you only do inference. Training of llama3.2 was done by its creators; you downloaded the result (a 2.0 GB file) with ollama pull llama3.2.

This split is the heart of AI engineering: someone else pays for training, you pay (in money or local compute) for every inference call.

What changes when you build on a model you did not train

In classic machine learning, the model is the product of your team's work. You gather data, design features, train, tune and deploy it. Building on a foundation model flips this around. The model arrives finished. You talk to it through an API: you send a request with some text, you get text back. Your work moves to three places.

  1. Evaluation becomes the hardest part. The model can answer anything, in free text, slightly differently each time. "Is this answer good?" no longer has a simple yes/no, and there are thousands of possible questions. Most of the labs (04, 05, 07) are about measuring quality.
  2. Prompts become code. The instructions you send with every request (the prompt) decide much of the behaviour. Small wording changes can change results, so prompts deserve versions and tests, just like source files. In the labs they live in a prompts/ folder.
  3. Context becomes the main lever. A general model knows nothing about Trailhead Bikes. The facts it needs must be put into the request at the moment you ask: relevant policy text, the customer's order, today's date. Finding and inserting the right information is a large part of the job (retrieval in lab 06, tools in lab 09).

Other things become simpler. You need no training data to get started, no GPU cluster, and a first prototype can take an afternoon. That is exactly why so many products appeared so quickly, and why many of them disappoint: a demo is easy, a reliable product is not.

The model as an API, in the labs

In lab 01 you call the model through one small function, chat(), in aie/llm.py. It uses the official openai Python package, but points it at Ollama on your own machine (http://localhost:11434/v1) instead of OpenAI's servers. This works because Ollama offers an OpenAI-compatible API: it accepts the same request format. Changing one setting (AIE_BASE_URL, AIE_MODEL) points the same code at a different provider or model. Here is the real output of lab 01:

Server: http://localhost:11434/v1   Model: llama3.2

An e-bike, also known as an electric bicycle, is a pedal-powered bicycle that is assisted by an electric motor, battery, and control system to provide a boost to the rider's pedaling efforts, making it easier to ride and often more efficient and environmentally friendly.

43 input tokens, 56 output tokens, 8.0 s

Notice the last line. The wrapper reports how many tokens went in, how many came out and how long it took. With a paid API, tokens are what you are billed for; with a local model, they are what your GPU spends its time on. AI engineers watch these numbers from the very first call, because cost and speed are part of quality.

New to APIs? What "calling a model through an API" means

An API (application programming interface) is an agreed way for one program to ask another for something. A web API works over HTTP, the same protocol your browser uses: your program sends a request to an address, with some data attached (here, the messages as JSON), and receives a response. You do not need to know how the other side works, only the format of the request and the response. Providers publish that format in their documentation.

A useful way to judge an idea is to ask how bad a wrong answer is. A brainstorming tool that suggests a bad shop name is harmless; a support bot that promises a free return the shop does not offer creates a real cost. The higher the cost of mistakes, the more evaluation, grounding and guardrails you need, and the more you may want a human to check answers before they reach customers.

Planning an AI application

Because a prototype is cheap, it is tempting to skip planning. A few questions up front save a lot of wasted effort later.

Is it worth building?

  • What problem does it solve, and for whom? "Customers wait two days for e-mail answers about returns" is a problem. "We should have a chatbot" is not.
  • What happens if you do nothing? Some projects defend against competitors, some save time, some are experiments to learn. Be honest about which one yours is.
  • Build or buy? If an existing product already does the job well, building your own only makes sense when you have something it lacks: your own data, your own workflow, or strict privacy needs. Trailhead's policies are private to the shop, which is why a custom bot that reads them makes sense.
  • Where does the human stay in the loop? The bot can draft answers for staff to approve, answer only simple questions, or answer everything. Starting with more human control is usually safer.

Setting expectations with numbers

Decide what "good enough" means before building, in terms you can measure. For a support bot that could be: the share of test questions answered correctly, how often it invents a policy, how long an answer takes, and how much each answer costs. In lab 04 you write 20 test questions and measure a bot that knows nothing about the shop. It passes the keyword check on about 30% of them and confidently invents things, such as a 5-year frame warranty and US shipping prices in dollars. That number is your baseline: every later improvement is measured against it.

Milestones: the last mile is the longest

Getting from nothing to an impressive demo is often quick. Getting from "impressive on the questions I tried" to "reliable on what real customers ask" usually takes far longer, because the remaining problems are rare cases, odd phrasings and failures you have not seen yet. Plan milestones around measurable quality, not features.

Maintenance: the ground moves under you

When you build on someone else's model, that model changes without asking you. Providers release new versions, change prices, retire old models and adjust how their APIs behave. Open models get new releases too. A prompt that worked well with one model may work worse with its successor. Two habits help:

  • Keep the model name in configuration, not scattered in code. In the labs it is one environment variable, AIE_MODEL, read in aie/config.py.
  • Keep an evaluation set you can re-run in minutes. Then "should we switch to the new model?" becomes a measurement instead of a guess.

The three layers of the AI stack

It helps to picture an AI application as three layers. Each layer has its own skills and its own kind of work.

  • Application layer: everything that turns a model into a product. Prompts, context and retrieval, tools and agents, evaluation, guardrails, the user interface, feedback collection. Most AI engineering happens here, and most of the labs live here.
  • Model layer: creating or changing models. Training, finetuning, building datasets, and making models smaller and faster to run (for example with quantization). Many teams never touch this layer, or only reach it later.
  • Infrastructure layer: what everything runs on. GPUs and servers, model serving, storage, monitoring and the systems that move data around. In the labs this is mostly Ollama on your machine; in a company it might be a cloud provider plus an internal platform team.

Nothing in the top layer is brand new. Prompting, evaluation and serving existed before foundation models. What is new is how much of the work they now represent, and that the top layer became reachable for developers without a machine learning background.

AI engineering, ML engineering and full-stack development

These job titles overlap and companies use them loosely. Still, the difference in emphasis is real and helps you see which skills to learn next.

Two trends are worth knowing. First, AI engineering often starts from the product: teams build a demo, show it to users, and only then invest in data and models if the idea works. Classic ML often started from data. Second, many AI engineers come from web and backend development rather than from machine learning, because so much of the work is software engineering around an API. The skill they most often need to add is evaluation: the habit of measuring quality instead of eyeballing a few answers.

New to these job titles? A one-line version of each

ML engineer: builds and runs models trained on the company's data. AI engineer: builds products on top of foundation models and makes them reliable. Full-stack developer: builds the whole web application, front end to database. In a small team, one person often does all three.

Common misconceptions
  • "It answered my five test questions, so it works." Five hand-picked questions say little. Lab 04 shows a bot that sounds confident and is wrong most of the time.
  • "A bigger model fixes everything." A bigger model still knows nothing about your shop's private policies. Missing context is a design problem, not a size problem.
  • "AI engineering means training models." Most of the work happens without changing any model weights.
  • "Once it works, it stays working." Models, prices and user questions change. Without a re-runnable eval set you will not notice when quality drops.
  • "Local models are toys." They are smaller and slower on a laptop, but the engineering (prompts, evaluation, retrieval, guardrails) is the same you would do with a large hosted model.
Recap
  • Self-supervision let language models learn from huge amounts of unlabelled text; scale made them general (LLMs); adding images, audio and video made them foundation models.
  • When you build on a model you did not train, it becomes an API call, and your work moves to evaluation, prompts and context.
  • Common applications include assistants, extraction, document Q&A, agents, writing and coding help. The cost of a wrong answer decides how much care you need.
  • Plan with measurable success criteria, a baseline, milestones based on quality, and a way to re-test when models change.
  • The stack has three layers: application, model, infrastructure. AI engineering lives mostly at the top.
