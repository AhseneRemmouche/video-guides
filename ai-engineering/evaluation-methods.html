Evaluation methods

Why evaluating open-ended output is hard, exact and similarity metrics, AI judges and comparing models. Read with book chapter 3.

Read with chapter 3 · Evaluation Methodology of the book. This page explains the ideas in its own words and links them to the labs.

A support bot that answers "30 days" when the policy says 30 days is right. One that answers "5 years" for a lifetime warranty is wrong. That sounds easy to check, until you try to do it for thousands of free-text answers, written in different words every time. This chapter is about the tools you can use to decide whether a model's output is good: cheap exact checks, similarity scores, language-model metrics, another model acting as a judge, and comparing models head to head.

You will learn what each method measures, what it misses, and how much it costs. Every method is illustrated with something that really happened in the labs, where a small local model (llama3.2) answers questions about a fictional bike shop and a larger model (gemma4) grades it.

Why this matters for your project. Every later lab asks "did this change make the bot better?" A new prompt, a retriever, a guardrail, a cache: none of these can be judged by reading three answers and saying "looks fine". You need a measurement you trust. In lab 04 you build the first one and discover it lies a little; in lab 05 you build a better one and measure how much you can trust it.

Why evaluating open-ended output is hard

Classic software is tested with exact expectations: add(2, 3) must return 5. Classic machine learning models usually have a small, fixed set of outputs (spam or not spam, one of ten digits), so you can count how often the prediction matches the label. A foundation model that writes free text breaks both habits.

  • Many right answers. "You have 30 days to return an item", "Returns are accepted within thirty days of delivery" and "30 days, full refund" are all correct. A test that expects one exact string rejects two of them.
  • Partly right answers. In the lab baseline, the bot said opened helmets cannot be returned (right) and then offered a store credit that does not exist (invented). Is that answer correct? It depends on your rules, and you have to write those rules down.
  • Answers that sound right. A fluent, confident answer is easy to accept when skimming. The baseline said the frame warranty lasts 5 years in perfectly good English. Only someone who knows the policy (lifetime) can see the error. This is hallucination, and it is exactly what evaluation must catch.
  • Randomness. With sampling, the same question can produce a different answer each time. One run is a sample, not the truth. (See lab 02.)
  • The task is open. General models can be asked anything, so there is no natural "complete" test set. You must decide which questions matter for your application.
  • Models get better than the testers. For hard tasks (a long legal summary, a tricky proof), checking an answer can take an expert as long as writing it.

Because of all this, there is no single perfect method. Instead you combine several, from cheap and strict to expensive and flexible. Keep this picture in mind for the rest of the page.

Language-model metrics: entropy, cross-entropy and perplexity

Before looking at answers, it helps to know the metrics people use for the model itself. A language model is trained to predict the next token. For every position in a text it gives a probability to each possible next token. These metrics all measure one thing: how surprised the model is by real text.

New to probabilities over tokens? What the model actually outputs

When the model reads "Returns are accepted within 30", it does not just pick the next word. Internally it produces a list like: "days" 0.92, "calendar" 0.03, "business" 0.02, and thousands of tiny probabilities for everything else. The numbers add up to 1. Sampling then picks one token from this list. The metrics below look at the probability the model gave to the token that actually came next in some real text.

Entropy: how unpredictable the data is

Entropy is a property of the text source, not of a model. It measures how much information each token carries, on average, in bits. If the next token is always obvious (a list of dates in a fixed format), entropy is low. If anything could come next (creative writing, random passwords), entropy is high. A useful way to feel it: entropy of 2 bits per token is as unpredictable as choosing uniformly between 4 options at every step.

Cross-entropy: how well a model predicts that data

Cross-entropy measures how surprised a particular model is by real text. For each real next token, take the probability the model gave it, compute -log2(p) (a probability of 1 gives 0 bits of surprise; 0.5 gives 1 bit; 0.25 gives 2 bits), and average over the text. Lower is better. It can never go below the true entropy of the data: no model can predict text better than the text is actually predictable. Training a language model is, in fact, minimising cross-entropy on its training data.

Perplexity: the same number, easier to read

Perplexity is cross-entropy turned back into "number of choices": perplexity = 2 ** cross_entropy (or e ** cross_entropy if natural logs were used; the result is the same). A perplexity of 4 means the model is, on average, as unsure as if it were picking between 4 equally likely tokens. Lower is better. Some papers report bits per character or bits per byte instead, which removes the dependence on how the text was split into tokens.

When these metrics are useful, and when they are not

  • Useful while training or adapting a model. If you finetune a model on your shop's documents, perplexity on held-out shop text should drop. It is cheap and needs no labels.
  • Useful for data work. Text with unusually low perplexity may have been in the training data already (a sign of contamination, see the next chapter). Text with very high perplexity is often garbage or another language, which helps data cleaning (dataset engineering).
  • Not comparable across tokenizers. Two models that split text into different tokens produce perplexities over different units. Compare bits per byte, or compare only models that share a tokenizer.
  • Weak for chat assistants. Post-training teaches a model to give helpful, safe answers rather than to imitate internet text, which can make its perplexity on raw text worse while the product gets better. Perplexity tells you nothing about whether "5 years" is the right warranty.
  • You need token probabilities. Some APIs return them (often called logprobs); others hide them. Check your provider's documentation. The labs do not use them.

For an application like the support bot, you care about the answers, so the rest of this page is about evaluating outputs.

Exact evaluation: checks with no judgement involved

An exact method gives the same verdict every time, with no human or model opinion involved. It is the first thing to try because it is fast, free and repeatable. Its weakness is that it only sees the surface of the text.

Exact match

Exact match compares the output with an expected answer, character for character (often after lowercasing and trimming spaces). It works well when the answer space is small: a classification label ("return", "warranty", "shipping"), a yes/no, a number, an order id. It fails for free text, where right answers vary in wording.

The trick that makes exact match useful for LLMs is to ask for a constrained output. In lab 03 the model returns JSON validated by a Pydantic model, so the intent field can be compared exactly with the expected intent. The lab also showed the limit: llama3.2 produced perfectly valid JSON with the wrong intent. Validity is an exact check of format; correctness is a separate check.

Functional correctness: run it and see

For some outputs, you can check the effect instead of the text. Generated code can be executed against unit tests; a generated SQL query can be run and its rows compared with the expected rows; an agent that books an appointment can be checked by looking at the calendar afterwards. The output is correct if it works, however it is written. Code benchmarks often report pass@k: the chance that at least one of k generated attempts passes all the tests.

The lab project uses functional checks for its own deterministic code: uv run pytest runs 8 offline tests. In lab 09 the trajectory check is also functional: it looks at which tools the agent actually called and flagged that search_docs was missing.

Keyword checks, and how they lie

The first metric of the lab is a keyword check. Each question in evals/qa.jsonl lists strings the answer must contain, and questions with no answer in the documents must get a refusal. Here is the whole check, from aie/evaluate.py:

REFUSALS = ("i don't know", "i do not know", "don't have information", "not able to find", "no information")


def is_refusal(text):
    return any(r in text.lower().replace("’", "'") for r in REFUSALS)


def keyword_pass(question, answer):
    """Answerable: every must_include string appears. Unanswerable: the answer says it doesn't know."""
    if not question["answerable"]:
        return is_refusal(answer)
    return all(word.lower() in answer.lower() for word in question["must_include"])

It is a substring test after lowercasing. On the baseline run (the bot with no documents) it gave 30 % (6 of 20). Reading the answers shows that this number is wrong in both directions:

QuestionRequiredWhat the bot said (excerpt)Keyword verdictTruth
q08 Do you deliver to Switzerland?not"Yes, we do deliver to Switzerland ..."passwrong: Switzerland is not served. The check found "not" inside the word "note" later in the answer.
q13 I am 178 cm tall. Which frame size?L"I would recommend our 17" frame size ..."passwrong: the answer is size L. After lowercasing, "l" appears in almost any sentence.

Two of the six passes are false and at least two fails are false, so the real baseline accuracy is not 30 %. Nothing here is a bug in Python; it is a measurement problem. Keyword checks are still worth keeping: they cost nothing, run in milliseconds, and catch gross regressions (if the RAG bot stops saying "30" for the return window, something broke). Just never treat them as the final word.

Similarity against a reference answer

If you have a reference answer written by a person, you can score how close the model's answer is to it. This handles some of the "many right wordings" problem, at the cost of needing references for every question.

Lexical similarity: shared words

Lexical metrics count overlapping words or word sequences (n-grams). Edit distance counts how many character edits turn one string into another. Older metrics from machine translation and summarisation, such as BLEU and ROUGE, are of this kind. They are cheap and need no model. Their problem is that overlap is not meaning: "returns are accepted within 30 days" and "returns are not accepted after 30 days" share most of their words. And a correct answer in different words ("you have a month") scores poorly.

Semantic similarity: shared meaning, via embeddings

An embedding model turns a text into a vector, a list of numbers, so that texts with similar meaning get nearby vectors. To compare an answer with a reference, embed both and compute their cosine similarity (close to 1 means similar direction, close to 0 means unrelated). The lab uses the same idea for retrieval in lab 06, with nomic-embed-text.

Practical points if you use semantic similarity:

  • You must pick a threshold ("above 0.8 counts as correct"). The right value depends on the embedding model and the task; set it by looking at labelled examples, not by guessing.
  • Scores from different embedding models are not comparable with each other.
  • Some embedding models need special prefixes. nomic-embed-text expects search_query: and search_document: in front of the text; forgetting them quietly lowers quality.
  • Your score now depends on another model. Changing it changes all your historical numbers.

AI as a judge

The most flexible automatic method is to ask a model to grade the answer, the way a teacher grades an essay. This is an AI judge (often called "LLM-as-a-judge"). It can read meaning, follow detailed instructions, and explain its verdict. It is also a model, with all the weaknesses of models.

How it works in the lab

The judge in aie/judge.py gets three things: the customer's question, the shop's reference document for that question, and the bot's answer. A system prompt (the rubric) says what "correct" means. The judge must reply with JSON matching a small Pydantic model, so the verdict can be read by code:

class Verdict(BaseModel):
    reasoning: str
    verdict: Literal["correct", "incorrect"]

Designing the rubric

The rubric is the most important part of a judge. Vague instructions ("is this a good answer?") give vague, unstable verdicts. Good rubrics share some habits:

  • Define the classes precisely. The lab rubric says an answer is correct when it matches the reference and "states no number, policy or fact that contradicts or is missing from the reference". That second clause is what catches invented store credits.
  • Cover the edge cases you know. It says what to do when no document covers the question (correct only if the bot declines or points to support), and that extra politeness is fine.
  • Prefer few classes. A binary verdict (correct / incorrect) or a short scale with a written description of each level is far more stable than "score 1 to 10". Two graders rarely agree on the difference between a 6 and a 7.
  • Ask for reasoning before the verdict. It tends to make verdicts more careful and gives you something to read when a verdict looks wrong.
  • Force a parseable output. Structured output with a fixed set of values means no regex parsing of free text.
  • Version it. A change to the rubric changes every score. Treat it like code, and never compare scores produced by different rubric versions.

Known biases of AI judges

Researchers have measured several systematic tendencies in model graders. You cannot remove them completely, but you can design around them.

  • Position bias. In pairwise comparisons, judges often prefer the answer shown first (or second). Mitigation: ask twice with the order swapped, and count a win only if it holds both ways.
  • Verbosity bias. Longer, more detailed-looking answers tend to get better grades, even when the extra length adds nothing or adds errors. Mitigation: tell the rubric that length is not a quality, and check scores against answer length.
  • Self-preference. A model may rate answers written by itself, or by models of its own family, more favourably. Mitigation: use a judge from a different family than the model being graded, or check this on labelled data.
  • Inconsistency. The same input can get different verdicts on different runs. The lab uses temperature 0 to reduce this; it does not guarantee identical output on every server.
  • Sensitivity to wording. Small rubric changes can shift many verdicts. This is why the rubric is versioned and calibrated.

Cost, speed and the need to calibrate

A judge is a model call per answer, often with a larger and slower model than the one being graded. On the lab machine (a 4 GB laptop GPU), gemma4 took about 13 s per verdict with reasoning disabled (AIE_REASONING_EFFORT=none), and roughly 95–110 s when it was allowed to think first. Twenty answers is minutes; thousands of answers on every prompt change adds up fast, whether you pay in time or in API fees. A common pattern: run cheap exact checks on everything, and the judge on a sample or only on cases the cheap checks cannot decide.

The bigger issue is trust. A judge produces a number, and the number looks precise. Before relying on it, you must check it against human labels. That is what lab 05 does: the guide author labelled 20 saved baseline answers by hand (4 of 20 correct), then both the judge and the keyword check graded the same answers.

q01  human=ok   judge=bad  keyword=ok    <-- judge disagrees with human
q16  human=ok   judge=bad  keyword=ok    <-- judge disagrees with human
q20  human=bad  judge=ok   keyword=bad   <-- judge disagrees with human

Agreement with human labels: judge 17/20 = 85%, keyword check 16/20 = 80%

Three lessons come out of these 20 rows:

  1. The judge is only slightly better than the keyword check here (17 vs 16), but it is right for better reasons: it did not fall for "note" or for the stray "l". The keyword check's agreement was partly luck.
  2. Disagreements are usually about the rule, not the model. For q01 and q16 the judge applied "no fact missing from the reference" strictly; the human decided a harmless invented detail was acceptable. Either the rubric or the human label should change, so that both express the same policy.
  3. A human label is also a judgement. Calibration is not "make the judge copy the human"; it is making the rule clear enough that careful humans and the judge agree.

Twenty labels are enough to find these issues, not enough to state "this judge is 85 % accurate" with confidence (the next chapter explains why). For a judge you rely on, label more examples, track agreement each time you change the rubric or the judge model, and re-check periodically.

Agreement beyond chance: why raw percent agreement can flatter a judge

In lab 05, 16 of the 20 human labels are "bad". A "judge" that always answered "bad" would agree with the human 80 % of the time without reading anything. So compare agreement with this trivial baseline. Statistics such as Cohen's kappa correct for chance agreement: 0 means "no better than chance", 1 means perfect agreement. You do not need to compute kappa to get the idea: always ask "how well would a dumb rule do on this data?"

Comparative evaluation: ranking models by preferences

Sometimes you do not need an absolute score; you need to know which of several models or prompts is better. Comparative evaluation shows two outputs for the same input to a judge (a person or a model) and asks which is better. Many such comparisons are then combined into a ranking.

Why compare instead of score? People are much better at saying "this one is better" than at assigning consistent numbers. Comparisons also keep working when all candidates are good: two strong answers may both get 9 out of 10, but a reader can still prefer one.

From match results to a ranking

The simplest summary is the win rate: how often model A beats model B. With several models, you want one score per model that explains all the match results. Two classic approaches, borrowed from sports and statistics:

  • Elo ratings (from chess). Each model has a rating. Before a match, the ratings predict the chance of winning: with ratings RA and RB, A is expected to win with probability 1 / (1 + 10 ** ((R_B - R_A) / 400)). After the match, the winner gains points and the loser loses the same amount, more when the result was a surprise. Ratings update one match at a time, so the order of matches matters a little.
  • Bradley–Terry model. Same idea of hidden strengths, but fitted to all comparisons at once, so the result does not depend on match order. It is what many public leaderboards based on human votes use today, often with confidence intervals around each score.

Limits of comparative evaluation

  • Relative, not absolute. The best of three bad bots is still bad. For a support bot you also need "is the answer correct?", which only absolute evaluation answers.
  • Many comparisons needed. The number of pairs grows quickly with the number of candidates, and each pair needs many votes before the difference is clear.
  • Preferences are not always consistent. A can beat B, B beat C and C beat A. Rating models assume a single strength per model, which smooths this over.
  • Who votes, on what, matters. Public leaderboards reflect the prompts and taste of their voters, which may be nothing like your customers' questions. Voters also tend to reward style (length, formatting, confidence), the same verbosity bias judges have.
  • Judging correctness needs knowledge. A voter who does not know the shop's return policy cannot tell which answer is correct, only which sounds better.

Comparative evaluation is great for choosing between prompt versions or models when both are plausible (you could run pairwise judging on the outputs of lab 08's two prompt versions), and as a complement to an absolute correctness check, not a replacement.

Pitfalls and common misconceptions
  • "The metric went up, so the bot got better." Only if the metric measures what you care about. The lab's keyword score rewarded "note" for containing "not".
  • "Low perplexity means good answers." Perplexity measures prediction of text, not correctness or helpfulness, and chat models are not optimised for it.
  • "Semantic similarity understands correctness." It mostly captures topic. "5-year warranty" and "lifetime warranty" are close in meaning-space and opposite in fact.
  • "A judge model is objective." It has biases (position, verbosity, self-preference) and follows its rubric literally. Measure its agreement with humans before trusting it.
  • "Raw percent agreement is enough." If most labels are "bad", a judge that always says "bad" also scores high. Compare with a trivial baseline.
  • Changing two things at once. New rubric and new judge model on the same day: you cannot tell which caused the score change. Version both.
  • Trusting one run. Model outputs vary. Small score differences between two runs on 20 questions are often noise.
Recap
  • Open-ended output has many right answers, partly right answers and convincing wrong ones, so no single method is enough.
  • Entropy, cross-entropy and perplexity measure how well a model predicts text. Useful for training and data work, not for judging a chat answer.
  • Exact methods (exact match, functional tests, keyword checks) are cheap and repeatable but surface-level. Lab 04's keyword check produced false passes ("not" in "note", "l" anywhere) and false fails.
  • Similarity to a reference: lexical overlap is cheap but blind to meaning; embedding similarity sees topic better but can miss a wrong number.
  • An AI judge reads meaning using a rubric. Design the rubric carefully, watch for position, verbosity and self-preference bias, and budget for the cost.
  • Always calibrate a judge against human labels: in lab 05 it agreed 17/20 times, and the three disagreements revealed rubric ambiguities, not just model errors.
  • Pairwise comparisons combined with Elo or Bradley–Terry give rankings: good for choosing between candidates, silent about whether any is good enough.
