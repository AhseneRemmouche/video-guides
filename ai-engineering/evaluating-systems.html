Evaluating an AI system

Evaluation criteria, choosing a model, and designing an evaluation pipeline you can trust. Read with book chapter 4.

Read with chapter 4 · Evaluate AI Systems of the book. This page explains the ideas in its own words and links them to the labs.

The previous chapter was about how to score one output: exact checks, similarity, AI judges, comparisons. This chapter zooms out to the whole application. What should you measure? How do you pick a model in the first place? Can you trust public benchmarks? And how do you build an evaluation pipeline that tells you, every time you change something, whether the product got better?

The running example is the Trailhead Bikes support bot. You will see how the labs already form a small evaluation pipeline: an eval set and a baseline (lab 04), a calibrated judge (lab 05), a retrieval evaluation (lab 06) and an end-to-end evaluation of the full RAG bot (lab 07).

Why this matters for your project. Teams often ship AI features after a few impressive demos, then discover in production that the bot invents prices or refuses easy questions. A good evaluation pipeline is what turns "it seemed fine" into "on our 20 questions it went from 30 % to 80 %, and here are the 4 it still gets wrong". It is also the only way to choose between models, prompts and retrievers without guessing.

Evaluation criteria: what "good" means for your application

Before choosing methods, decide what you are measuring. "Is the bot good?" is too vague to test. Break it into criteria that each have a clear question and, ideally, a clear check. Four families cover most applications.

Domain capability

Can the model do the core task of your domain? For a coding assistant that means writing code that runs; for a translation tool, translating; for the support bot, knowing the shop's policies and applying them to a question ("I am 178 cm tall, which frame size?"). A general model can be strong at reasoning and still know nothing about your shop: the lab baseline confidently invented a 5-year frame warranty and US shipping prices in dollars. Domain capability can come from the model itself, from finetuning, or from context you provide at question time, which is exactly what RAG does.

Domain capability is usually checked with questions that have known answers, scored by exact methods or a reference-based judge. If the task is code or SQL, functional correctness (run it) is best.

Generation quality: faithful, relevant and safe

For free-text answers, "correct" splits into several sub-questions:

  • Factual consistency. Does the answer agree with the facts? There are two versions. Local consistency: does it agree with the context it was given (the retrieved chunks)? This is often called faithfulness or grounding. Global consistency: does it agree with the real world? For a support bot, local consistency is what you control and can check: the lab judge compares the answer with the shop's reference document.
  • Relevance. Does it answer the question that was asked? "We recommend a full service every 6–12 months" (lab 04, q17) is a fine sentence and does not answer "when should I drop off my bike?".
  • Fluency and coherence. Is it readable and logically organised? Modern models rarely fail here, so this criterion matters less than it used to.
  • Safety. Does it avoid harmful content: toxic language, dangerous instructions, leaking personal data, following injected instructions? Safety is usually checked with dedicated classifiers, lists of known attack prompts and guardrails (see lab 10 and lab 08 on prompt injection).
New to "faithfulness"? Correct and faithful are not the same thing

Imagine the retriever returned the wrong document, and the bot answered correctly from its own memory. The answer is correct but not faithful to the context. Now imagine the retrieved document is outdated and the bot repeats it: faithful but not correct. For a support bot you usually want both, and you want the bot to prefer the documents over its memory, because the documents are what the shop can update and stand behind. That is why the lab's rag_v2 prompt says "Answer only with facts from the context below. Do not use outside knowledge."

Instruction following

Does the model do what the prompt asks, in the form it asks? This includes output format (valid JSON with the right fields, as in lab 03), length limits, language, tone, and specific rules. Many instruction-following checks are cheap and exact: parse the JSON, count sentences, check that citations exist.

The lab 07 run gives a nice real example. Rule 2 of prompts/rag_v2.txt says that without an answer in the context, the bot must reply exactly "I don't know. Please contact support@trailhead.example." Rule 3 says to cite chunk ids. For q19 the bot replied "I don't know. Please contact support@trailhead.example. [service-plans#0]": it declined correctly but added a citation to a refusal, so the reply is not exactly the required text. A strict exact-match check on refusals would fail it; the keyword check (which only looks for "i don't know") passes it. Neither is wrong; you have to decide which rule you care about.

Cost and latency

A perfect answer that takes a minute or costs too much per conversation is not a product. Measure, for every run: input and output tokens (cost on paid APIs grows with both, and output tokens are usually priced higher), total time, and for chat interfaces the time to first token and time per output token. The lab's single model wrapper, aie/llm.py, records tokens and seconds on every call, so every evaluation run also reports cost and latency for free.

Adding retrieved chunks multiplied the input by about six (63 to 372 tokens per question). On a paid API that is a real cost increase. In this local run the RAG answers were even faster on average, likely because the grounded answers are short and to the point, while the baseline wrote longer invented explanations. That trade-off only became visible because the evaluation recorded cost and time next to quality. Lab 14 goes deeper into latency.

Choosing a model

There are many models, and new ones appear every month. You cannot evaluate them all carefully. A practical process narrows the field in stages, from cheap filters to expensive tests on your own data.

Hard requirements versus soft requirements

Hard requirements are pass/fail. If a model fails one, no score can rescue it. Examples: the data must not leave your servers; the licence must allow commercial use; the model must support French; the context must fit your longest documents; the model must run on the hardware you have. In the labs, a hard requirement was "free and local", which is why everything runs on Ollama on a laptop GPU.

Soft requirements are things you can trade against each other: accuracy, latency, cost, ease of use. A slightly less accurate model that is three times cheaper may be the right choice. The lab made exactly such a trade: llama3.2 (3B, about 2 GB) is the app model because it answers in seconds on a 4 GB GPU, while the larger and much slower gemma4 is used only as a judge, where quality matters more than speed.

Open-weight models versus proprietary APIs

One of the first big decisions is whether to call a model through a provider's API or run a model whose weights you can download (often called "open" models, although many have licences with restrictions; "open-weight" is more precise). Neither is always better. The trade-offs:

ConcernRun an open-weight model yourselfUse a proprietary model via API
Data privacyData can stay on your machines. Often decisive for medical, legal or internal data.Data goes to the provider. Read their data-use and retention terms; some offer stronger guarantees on business plans.
ControlYou choose the exact version, can pin it forever, inspect and finetune it, and change how it is served.The provider can update or retire a model. You get the features and limits they expose.
CostYou pay for hardware and engineering time, not per token. Cheap at steady high volume; expensive to operate well.Pay per token, nothing to run. Cheap to start and at low volume; can grow large at scale.
PerformanceStrong and improving, but the largest frontier models are often API-only. Speed depends on your hardware (the lab's T500 GPU is slow).Often the most capable models, on fast infrastructure. Rate limits and network latency apply.
EffortYou handle serving, scaling, monitoring, security updates.Mostly a key and an SDK.

The lab keeps both doors open: every call goes through the OpenAI-compatible API of Ollama, so switching to a hosted provider is a change of a few settings in .env (base URL, key, model names). Building on a common interface is a cheap way to keep model choice a reversible decision. Any comparison you make (prices, which model is strongest) is a snapshot "at the time of writing"; check current documentation before deciding.

Public benchmarks and their limits

A benchmark is a public eval set with a fixed scoring method, used to compare models: multiple-choice knowledge questions, maths word problems, coding tasks with unit tests, and many more. Leaderboards collect scores across several benchmarks. They are useful for building a first shortlist, especially for general skills like coding or reasoning. They are risky as a final answer, for several reasons.

  • Contamination. Models are trained on huge web crawls. If a benchmark's questions (and answers) were published online, they may be in the training data, and the model may have memorised them. Its score then measures memory, not skill. Contamination is often accidental and hard to rule out; some benchmark makers keep a private test set for this reason.
  • Saturation. When top models all score near the maximum, the benchmark can no longer tell them apart. The field then moves to harder benchmarks, which saturate in turn.
  • Mismatch with your task. No public benchmark contains the Trailhead return policy. A model can top a general knowledge benchmark and still invent a 5-year warranty for your shop.
  • Setup differences. Scores depend on the prompt, the number of examples in the prompt, sampling settings and how answers are extracted. Two reports of "the same" benchmark are not always comparable; self-reported numbers may use the most favourable setup.
  • Over-optimisation. When a number becomes a target, people tune for it. Improvements on a famous benchmark may not carry over to real use.

How to use benchmarks sensibly: pick the ones closest to your task, look at several rather than one, prefer evaluations run by independent parties with published settings, and treat differences of a point or two as noise. Then run your shortlist on your own eval set. The lab's 20 questions in evals/qa.jsonl are tiny next to a public benchmark, but they test exactly what Trailhead's customers ask.

Designing an evaluation pipeline

An evaluation pipeline is the repeatable process that scores your system every time something changes. It is not one script; it is a set of decisions. Here they are in a practical order, each tied to what the labs did.

Evaluate each component, not only the end result

A RAG bot is a chain: a retriever finds chunks, then a model writes an answer from them. If the final answer is wrong, which link failed? End-to-end scores cannot tell you. So evaluate each component with a metric suited to it, then the whole.

The real runs show why this matters. In lab 07 the full bot passed 16 of 20 questions. The four failures look alike from outside, but they are not:

  • q06 (frame number): lab 06 had already shown that hybrid and embedding retrieval return chunks from the sizing document for this question. The model then answered from the wrong context. Fix the retriever (or the chunking), not the prompt.
  • q08 (Switzerland) and q16 (Annecy on Monday): the retriever found the right document, and the bot still replied "I don't know". The shipping document lists the countries served but never says "not Switzerland", and the stores document says "all stores" are closed on Monday without naming Annecy in that sentence. Each needed one small step of reasoning, which the strict "answer only with facts from the context" rule probably discouraged in a small model. This is a generation problem: rewording the prompt or using a stronger model are the fixes to try, and lab 08 compares prompt versions.
  • q03 (return shipping): the answer "free for orders over 100 EUR" is correct but incomplete; the keyword check also wanted the 6.90 EUR fee for smaller orders. Whether that is a failure depends on your guidelines.

Component metrics also make each part cheaper to improve. Retrieval metrics like hit rate and MRR need no model call at all, so you can try ten chunking settings in seconds before running the slow end-to-end evaluation. The same principle applies to agents: lab 09 checks the trajectory (which tools were called) separately from the final answer.

Write evaluation guidelines and a rubric

The hardest part of evaluation is not code; it is agreeing on what a good answer is. Write it down, with examples. For the support bot, guidelines would answer questions like: Is an answer that is right but adds a harmless invented detail correct? (Lab 05's human said yes for q16, the judge said no.) Is a correct but incomplete answer acceptable? (q03 above.) Should the bot say "no" to "do you sell skateboards?" or decline? (The human and the judge disagreed on q20.) What tone is expected? Each answer becomes a line in the judge's rubric and an instruction for human labellers. Guidelines are also how you discover that your team disagrees about the product, which is better learned before launch.

Tie evaluation metrics to business metrics

The company does not care about "keyword pass rate". It cares about things like the share of customer questions resolved without a human, support costs, customer satisfaction, or return requests handled correctly. Connect the two: if the bot answers correctly 80 % of the time, how many tickets does that save, and how much damage do the wrong 20 % do? Some errors are worse than others: inventing a free-return promise costs money; saying "I don't know" just sends the customer to support. This mapping tells you which criterion to prioritise, and what score is "good enough to ship". It also tells you what to watch after launch with user feedback (lab 12).

For data, use real questions whenever you can (from support tickets, search logs, beta users), plus hand-written cases for situations real data rarely shows: unanswerable questions, edge cases, attacks. Each row should store what you need for every method: the question, the expected key facts, the source document and whether it is answerable, as each line of evals/qa.jsonl does. Synthetic data can extend the set (lab 13), but check it by hand, and keep a core of human-written examples.

Slice your data

An average hides who is failing. Split the results by meaningful groups (slices): question topic, answerable versus unanswerable, short versus long questions, language, customer type. Check the evaluation itself

Your evaluation is a measuring instrument, and instruments can be broken. Before trusting a score, check:

  • Are the test items right? Lab 04 showed bad items: must_include: ["L"] passes almost any sentence, and ["not"] passes "note". Fix the item, not the bot. Reading the answers behind a score is the cheapest audit there is.
  • Do graders agree? Compare the judge with humans (lab 05: 17 of 20), and ideally two humans with each other. If careful humans disagree often, the guidelines are unclear.
  • Is it reliable from run to run? Run the same evaluation twice without changing anything. If the score moves by 10 points, a 5-point "improvement" means nothing. Outputs vary between runs whenever you sample with a temperature above 0 (see lab 02), and even temperature 0 does not guarantee identical output on every server or model version.
  • Does it correlate with what matters? Over time, check that systems with better eval scores also do better on the business metric and with real users.
  • Is everything versioned? Eval set, rubric, judge model, prompts. The labs keep prompts as files in prompts/ and save every run to runs/<name>.jsonl, so any score can be traced back to the exact answers that produced it.

How many examples do you need?

Enough that the difference you care about is bigger than the noise. With 20 questions, one question is 5 percentage points. The lab's jump from 30 % to 80 % is far bigger than that, so it is clearly real. A change from 80 % to 85 % is a single question, and could easily flip back on the next run.

A standard statistics result gives a feel for the numbers. If the true pass rate is around 50 % (the worst case), the uncertainty of a measured pass rate on n independent questions is roughly ± 1.96 × sqrt(0.25 / n) at the usual 95 % confidence level:

Rules of thumb (not laws), commonly used in practice:

  • Start small, start now. 20 to 50 well-chosen questions, read by a human, already catch most big problems. The lab's 20 found invented warranties, bad test items and three rubric ambiguities.
  • Grow towards a few hundred before you trust differences of a few points, and more per slice if you need conclusions about each slice.
  • Check stability with resampling. Re-compute the score on random subsets of your questions (bootstrapping). If the ranking of two systems flips often, you need more data.
  • Quality beats quantity. 100 correct, varied questions are worth more than 1,000 near-duplicates with sloppy references.
Pitfalls and common misconceptions
  • "The model is top of a leaderboard, so it will work for us." Leaderboards can be contaminated, saturated and unrelated to your task. Decide on your own eval set.
  • Only measuring end to end. A wrong answer can come from retrieval or from generation. Without component metrics you fix the wrong part.
  • One number for everything. Correctness, format, safety, cost and latency are different criteria. Average them and you lose the trade-offs.
  • Forgetting cost and latency. RAG multiplied input tokens by about six in the lab. That matters on a paid API, even when quality goes up.
  • Trusting small differences. On 20 questions, one question is 5 points. Do not celebrate or panic over a single question.
  • Never auditing the eval set. Bad items ("L" anywhere) quietly reward wrong answers. Read samples of passes and fails.
  • Letting the eval set leak. If you tune prompts by looking at the same questions you report on, scores become optimistic. Keep some questions aside that you only use for final checks.
  • "Open-weight is always cheaper" (or "APIs are always better"). It depends on volume, privacy needs, hardware and team. Re-check prices and capabilities when you decide; they change quickly.
Recap
  • Define criteria before methods: domain capability, generation quality (factual consistency, relevance, safety), instruction following, cost and latency.
  • Choose models in stages: hard requirements filter, public information for a shortlist, your own eval set for the decision. Weigh open-weight against API on privacy, control, cost and performance.
  • Public benchmarks suffer from contamination, saturation and mismatch with your task; use them only for shortlisting.
  • Evaluate each component: lab 06 scored retrieval (hybrid hit@3 94 %), lab 07 scored answers (80 % vs a 30 % baseline), and together they separate retrieval failures (q06) from generation failures (q08, q16).
  • Write guidelines and a rubric, connect metrics to business outcomes, slice the results, and check the evaluation itself: test items, agreement, run-to-run noise, versioning.
  • Small eval sets catch big problems; to trust small differences you need hundreds of questions (rule of thumb).
