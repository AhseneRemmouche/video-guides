How foundation models work

Training data, the transformer, post-training, sampling and why models hallucinate. Read with book chapter 2.

Read with chapter 2 · Understanding Foundation Models of the book. This page explains the ideas in its own words and links them to the labs.

You can build an AI application without knowing how a transformer computes attention, just as you can build a web app without knowing how a CPU executes instructions. But a rough picture of what happens inside the model explains a lot of behaviour that otherwise looks random: why the same question gets different answers, why a model invents a warranty that does not exist, why asking for JSON sometimes still goes wrong, and why one model takes ten times longer than another to answer.

This page walks through the life of a model: the data it learns from, how text becomes tokens, the transformer architecture, what "size" means, how a raw model is turned into a helpful assistant, and how it picks each word when it answers. It ends with structured outputs and hallucinations, two topics you meet directly in the labs.

Why this matters for your project. In lab 02 you change temperature and seed and watch the answers change. In lab 03 you force the model to answer in a JSON format and see that valid JSON can still be wrong. Both labs only make sense once you know how a model chooses its next token.

The life of a model, in one picture

A modern chat model is made in stages. First, pre-training on a huge pile of text produces a base model that is very good at continuing text. Then post-training teaches it to behave like an assistant. Finally, at inference time, a sampling step decides which words actually come out. You control only the last stage directly; the first two were done by the model's creators, but their choices shape everything you see.

Training data shapes what a model knows

A model can only learn patterns that appear in its training data. That sounds obvious, but its consequences show up everywhere in practice.

  • Domains. Most training text comes from the public web, plus books, code and other collected sources. Topics that are common there (programming, general knowledge, popular products) are well covered. Topics that are rare, specialised or private are not. Your company's internal documents were never in the training data at all.
  • Languages. Some languages, English above all, are heavily represented online; many others have far less text. Models are usually noticeably better in the well-represented languages. They often also need more tokens to write the same sentence in a less-represented language, which makes those answers slower and more expensive.
  • Time. Training data is collected up to some date, the training cutoff. The model knows nothing that happened later unless you tell it in the prompt.
  • Quality and filtering. Creators filter out spam, duplicates and harmful content, and choose how much of each source to include. These choices are part of why two models of similar size behave differently.

You saw the effect of training data in lab 04. Asked about a shop it has never heard of, llama3.2 did not say "I don't know". It answered with what a typical online shop might say: standard shipping in dollars, a multi-year frame warranty, deliveries to Switzerland. Those answers sound plausible precisely because they resemble a lot of text the model has seen. They are just not Trailhead's policies.

New to the idea of "knowledge in a model"? What the model actually stores

A model does not keep a copy of its training documents that it looks things up in. Training adjusts its parameters so that likely continuations get high probability. Facts that appear often end up "baked in" as strong patterns; facts that appear rarely may be fuzzy or mixed up with similar facts. This is why models can be fluent and wrong at the same time, and why, for private or changing information, you give the model the facts in the prompt instead of hoping it remembers them.

Tokens and tokenizers

Models do not read letters or words. A tokenizer first cuts text into tokens, chunks from a fixed vocabulary, and replaces each one with a number (its ID). Common words are usually one token. Rare words, names and numbers are split into several pieces. Each model family has its own tokenizer, so the same sentence can be a different number of tokens for different models.

Why not just use whole words? A vocabulary of every word in every language would be enormous, and unknown words would be impossible to represent. Why not single characters? Sequences would become very long, and the model would waste effort learning spelling. Sub-word tokens are a compromise: a vocabulary of tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand pieces that can spell out any text.

Tokens matter to you in three practical ways. You pay per token with hosted APIs. Generation time grows with the number of output tokens, because they are produced one at a time. And each model has a context window, a maximum number of tokens it can handle in one request (prompt plus answer). In lab 01, a one-sentence system message and the question "What is an e-bike?" counted as 43 input tokens. That is more than the words you typed, because the server wraps each message in special role markers before the model sees it (more on that below, under post-training).

The transformer and attention

Almost all of today's language models use an architecture called the transformer, introduced by researchers in 2017. You do not need the maths to use one. Here is the idea in four steps.

  1. Embed. Each token ID is turned into a long list of numbers (a vector) that the model learned during training. Tokens with related meanings end up with similar vectors.
  2. Mix with attention. In an attention step, every token looks at the earlier tokens and decides which ones are relevant to it, then blends in information from them. This is how "it" in a sentence picks up what "it" refers to.
  3. Process. A feed-forward step transforms each token's vector on its own. Attention plus feed-forward make one layer (a transformer block). Models stack dozens of these layers.
  4. Predict. At the end, the vector of the last position is turned into a score for every token in the vocabulary: how well each would fit as the next token. Those scores are called logits.

Two consequences are useful to remember. First, every token attends to every earlier token, so the work grows quickly with prompt length; long prompts are slower and need more memory. Second, generation is autoregressive: the model produces one token, appends it to the input, and runs again to get the next. An answer of 56 tokens (like lab 01's) means 56 rounds of this loop. Servers save time by caching intermediate results of earlier tokens (the KV cache), a topic for inference optimization.

New to "architecture"? Do I need to choose one?

An architecture is the design of the computation: which steps happen, in which order, how they connect. The transformer is the dominant one for language models. Researchers keep proposing alternatives and variations, and some production models mix in other designs. As an application builder, you mostly pick models, not architectures; what you notice is the effect (speed, context length, memory use), which model cards and provider docs describe.

Model size: parameters, data and compute

Three numbers describe how "big" a model is.

  • Parameters: how many learned numbers the model has. More parameters can store more patterns, but every token you generate must pass through them, so bigger models are slower and need more memory to run. llama3.2 in the labs has about 3 billion; frontier hosted models are much larger.
  • Training tokens: how much data the model saw during pre-training. A model with many parameters but too little data is under-trained; a small model trained on a lot of data can punch above its size.
  • Compute: the total amount of calculation spent on training, usually counted in floating-point operations (FLOPs). It grows with both parameters and training tokens; a common rough estimate is 6 × parameters × training tokens. Compute is what costs money and time.

A quick calculation shows why model files are smaller than you might expect. Stored at 16 bits (2 bytes) per parameter, 3 billion parameters would take about 6 GB. The llama3.2 download is 2.0 GB, which means its numbers are stored with fewer bits each, roughly 5 on average. Storing weights with fewer bits is called quantization; it is what lets a 3-billion-parameter model fit on the 4 GB laptop GPU used to test these labs, at a small cost in quality.

Some models use a mixture of experts design: they have many parameters in total, but only a part of them is used for each token. For these models, look at both the total and the "active" parameter count; the active count decides speed, the total decides memory.

Bigger is not automatically better for your app. In the labs, gemma4 is larger and more accurate (it got all three tickets right in lab 03 where llama3.2 did not), but it is also much slower on a small GPU. The right size is the smallest model that passes your evaluation at an acceptable speed and cost. Model sizes and rankings change constantly, so compare current models on your own eval set rather than trusting a leaderboard.

Post-training: from text completer to assistant

A base model straight out of pre-training is a text completer. Give it "What is your return policy?" and it may continue with more questions, as if writing a FAQ page, because that is a likely continuation on the web. It has knowledge but no notion of "a user asked me something and I should answer helpfully".

Post-training fixes this in two main steps.

  1. Supervised finetuning (SFT). The model is trained further on example conversations: an instruction and a high-quality answer, written or checked by people. It learns the format of a helpful reply, how to follow instructions, and the chat format with roles (system, user, assistant).
  2. Preference finetuning. People (or models) compare two answers to the same prompt and say which is better. The model is then pushed toward the preferred kind of answer. Common methods include reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which trains a separate "reward model" to score answers, and direct preference optimization (DPO), which learns from the comparisons directly.

This explains several things you will notice in the labs:

  • Roles and chat templates. Chat models were trained on conversations marked up with special tokens for "system", "user" and "assistant". The server converts your list of messages into that format. That is why the labs send {"role": "system", ...} and {"role": "user", ...} messages, and part of why a short question costs dozens of input tokens.
  • Style. Politeness, refusals, a habit of adding "However, ..." caveats, and a tendency to answer at length all come largely from post-training. The lab 04 baseline answers often end with an offer to help further; that is learned style, not knowledge.
  • Confidence. If training rewards answers that people like, and people like confident, complete answers, the model can learn to sound sure even when it has no basis. More on this under hallucination.

The models in the labs are already post-trained chat models. You will not post-train anything, but Finetuning and lab 13 show how the same idea applies when you adapt a model to your own data.

Sampling: how the model picks the next token

At every step the model produces a logit (a raw score) for each token in its vocabulary. A function called softmax turns these scores into probabilities that add up to 1. Then the server has to pick one token. Always taking the most likely one is called greedy decoding. It is predictable but can be bland or repetitive. Picking at random according to the probabilities is sampling. The settings below shape that random choice.

Temperature

Temperature divides the logits before softmax. A low temperature (below 1) widens the gaps between scores, so the top token becomes even more likely. A high temperature (above 1) shrinks the gaps, so unlikely tokens get a real chance. Temperature 0 is treated as "always take the top token", which is greedy decoding.

Lab 02 asks llama3.2 for a bike shop name six times at each of three temperatures, then twice more with a fixed seed. Here is the real run:

Top-k and top-p

High temperatures give a small chance to thousands of nonsense tokens. Two filters cut off that long tail before sampling. Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens. Top-p (also called nucleus sampling) keeps the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, so the number of candidates adapts: few when the model is sure, more when it is not.

Seeds: repeatable randomness

Computers produce "random" numbers from a starting value called a seed. Use the same seed, the same model, the same prompt and the same settings, and the server makes the same random choices. In lab 02, two calls at temperature 1.0 with seed=42seed: 42 both returned Gearhaven, in the Python run and in the TypeScript run. Seeds are great for debugging and for repeatable experiments. Do not rely on them across machines, server versions or providers: hardware differences and server optimisations such as batching can still change results, and some hosted APIs treat the seed as best effort only.

Test-time compute and reasoning models

Another way to get better answers is to spend more computation at inference time instead of training a bigger model. One simple version: generate several answers and pick the best one, using a checker, a scoring model or a majority vote. Another is to let the model "think out loud" before answering: produce a chain of intermediate reasoning, then the final answer. Models trained to do this by default are called reasoning models (or "thinking" models). The thinking tokens are real generated tokens: they take time, and with hosted APIs they are usually billed, even if they are hidden from the final reply.

The labs ran into this directly. gemma4, the judge model, is a thinking model. With thinking left on, one answer took about 95–110 seconds on the test machine, spending roughly 350–400 hidden thinking tokens. Passing reasoning_effort="none"reasoning_effort: "none" brought the same kind of answer down to about 13 seconds and 32 tokens. That is why aie/config.pysrc/config.ts sets AIE_REASONING_EFFORT to none by default and aie/llm.pysrc/llm.ts adds it to every request.

Structured outputs

Applications often need data, not prose: a support ticket with an intent, an order number and an urgency flag. You want structured output, usually JSON that matches a JSON Schema. There are three layers of defence, and lab 03 uses all of them.

  1. Ask in the prompt. "Reply with JSON only" plus a description of the fields. Usually works, sometimes adds a sentence before the JSON or forgets a field.
  2. Constrain the decoding. The server receives the schema and, at every step, blocks tokens that would break it. If the JSON so far is {"intent": " and the schema allows only five values, every token that cannot start one of them gets probability zero before sampling. The output is then guaranteed to parse. In the labs, chat_json()chatJson() sends the schema as response_format with type json_schema; Ollama supports this through its structured-outputs feature.
  3. Validate and retry. After the reply arrives, Pydantic checks it against the Ticket modelZod checks it against the Ticket schema, including rules the decoder may not enforce, like the order-id pattern ^TH-\d{5}$. If validation fails, chat_json()chatJson() sends the error back and asks for a fix, up to two retries.

Hallucination: confident answers without a basis

A hallucination is an answer that sounds right but is not supported by facts or by the information the model was given. The lab 04 baseline is full of them: a 5-year frame warranty, shipping prices in dollars, deliveries to Switzerland. None of these exist in Trailhead's documents. Why does a model do this?

  • It was trained to continue text plausibly, not truthfully. A fluent, typical answer is exactly what pre-training rewards. "Our frames have a 5-year warranty" is a very typical sentence for a bike shop.
  • It does not know what it does not know. Nothing in the generation loop checks a fact against a source. The model has no separate signal that says "this part is a guess", and its wording sounds equally sure either way.
  • It builds on its own output. Each generated token becomes input for the next one. Once the model has written "Yes, we deliver to", the most plausible continuation is a country name and a confident explanation, not a retraction.
  • Training can reward guessing. If post-training examples always contain an answer, the model learns that answering is what is expected, even about things it never saw.
  • The facts are missing. Private, recent or very specific information was never in the training data. Here, that is all of Trailhead's policies.

You cannot remove hallucinations completely, but you can make them much rarer and easier to catch:

Pitfalls and misconceptions
  • "Temperature 0 makes the model correct." It makes it consistent. In lab 03 the model was wrong at temperature 0, the same way every time.
  • "Temperature 0 is always perfectly deterministic." Usually close, but servers, hardware and batching can still cause small differences. Use a seed for repeatable experiments and still expect occasional drift.
  • "JSON mode means the data is right." It means the data parses. Lab 03 produced valid JSON with invented order ids.
  • "The model knows it is unsure." Its tone carries no reliable information about accuracy. Measure instead.
  • "Thinking models are always better." They can be, on hard problems, at a large cost in time and tokens. For the lab's judge, thinking turned a 13-second answer into a wait of over a minute and a half.
  • "Words and tokens are the same." Limits and costs are in tokens; rare words, names and some languages use many tokens per word.
Recap
  • A chat model is pre-trained on huge amounts of text (knowledge), then post-trained with SFT and preference finetuning (behaviour). Sampling at inference time picks the words you get.
  • Training data decides which domains and languages a model handles well; private and recent facts are missing.
  • Text becomes tokens; tokens drive cost, speed and the context window.
  • Transformers mix information between tokens with attention and generate one token per loop.
  • Size means parameters, training tokens and compute; quantization makes models smaller to run.
  • Temperature, top-k, top-p and seeds shape sampling; reasoning models spend extra tokens thinking.
  • Constrained decoding guarantees JSON shape, not content. Hallucinations come from plausible-text training and missing facts; grounding, tools, checks and evaluation reduce them.
