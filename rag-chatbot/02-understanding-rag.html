Understanding Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

Retrieval, augmentation and generation explained.

▶ Watch this part (01:03)

A concrete scenario

Say you are building an internal chatbot so employees can ask about company policies, benefits and procedures. The AI model has never seen your company's data. It doesn't know your vacation policy, your expense reimbursement process or anything else internal.

So when someone asks "What's our vacation policy?" or "How many sick days do I get?", a normal chatbot can't answer. It simply doesn't have that information.

Worse, it may guess. A language model is built to produce fluent, plausible text, so instead of saying "I don't know" it can invent a confident but wrong answer such as "You get 20 vacation days". This is often called a hallucination. RAG reduces it by giving the model the real text to answer from.

New to LLMs? What a large language model actually does

A large language model (LLM), such as OpenAI's GPT models, is a program trained on an enormous amount of text. During training it learned one skill extremely well: given some text, predict what text most likely comes next.

When you chat with it, your app sends the conversation so far, and the model continues it, one small piece at a time, until the answer is complete. Everything it "knows" comes from two places only:

  1. Its training data, which was frozen at a certain date and never included your private files.
  2. Whatever you put in the request right now: the prompt, the earlier messages and any extra text you add.

The model has no memory between requests and cannot browse your company drive by itself. RAG works entirely through point 2: it puts the right text into the request.

New to tokens and context windows? Why you can't just paste every document in

Models don't read letters or whole words. They read tokens: small chunks of text, often a word or part of a word. As a rough rule for English, 1 token is about 4 characters or three quarters of a word. "What is our vacation policy?" is about 7 tokens.

Every model has a context window: the maximum number of tokens it can look at in one request (your question, the conversation history, any documents you add, and its own answer). You are also billed per token, and longer requests are slower.

So the naive idea of "paste the entire handbook, every policy and every manual into every question" breaks down: it may not fit, it costs a lot on every single question, and the important sentence gets buried. RAG sends only the few passages that matter for this question.

What RAG stands for

WordMeaning in this project
RetrievalSearch your own data sources for relevant information and fetch it. Here that means a vector similarity search in Neon.
AugmentedAdd what you fetched to what the AI sees. The search results go back to the model as the output of a tool call.
GenerationThe AI writes its answer from both its training and the information you supplied.

The AI doesn't have to remember everything. You give it access to the company's knowledge base. When a question comes in, you quickly find the relevant documents and hand them to the AI, and it uses them to give an accurate answer.

A good everyday comparison is an open-book exam. A student who must answer from memory may misremember. A student who can look up the right page in the textbook first, then write the answer in their own words, gets it right. RAG turns the model's closed-book exam into an open-book one, and your app is the one that finds the right page.

Walking through the diagram below with the vacation question:

  1. Retrieve: your app searches the stored chunks of your PDFs and picks the 3 that are closest in meaning to "What is our vacation policy?", for example the paragraph that starts "Full-time employees accrue 1.5 days of paid leave per month…".
  2. Augment: those 3 chunks are added to what the model sees, next to the user's question.
  3. Generate: the model writes a normal, friendly answer based on that text.

Why it matters

  • Updates show up immediately. If you changed the vacation policy last week or added a new benefits package, upload the new document and the bot knows it straight away. No retraining.
  • Grounded answers. The model answers from your actual documents instead of guessing.
  • A knowledge assistant. The AI can draw on all your current documentation.

Compare this with the other way to teach a model new facts, fine-tuning (training the model further on your data). Fine-tuning is slow, costs money every time the documents change, and still doesn't guarantee the model repeats the exact facts. With RAG, changing the knowledge is as easy as uploading a new PDF.

The key ideas you will implement

Embedding
A list of numbers (here 1,536 of them) that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions. You build this in step 10. See embedding.
Chunking
Splitting a long document into small, slightly overlapping pieces, so retrieval can return just the relevant passage instead of the whole PDF. You build this in step 11. See chunk.
Vector database
A database that stores the embeddings and can quickly find the ones closest to a query's embedding. Here that is Neon Postgres with pgvector, set up in step 9.
Semantic search
Search by meaning using cosine similarity, not by matching keywords. You build this in step 14. See semantic search.
Tool calling
The model decides for itself when to call your searchKnowledgeBase function, then answers from the results. You build this in step 15. See tool calling.
New to embeddings? An intuitive picture of "meaning as numbers"

Imagine describing every sentence with just two scores: "how much is it about time off?" and "how much is it about money?". Then each sentence becomes a point on a map:

"How many vacation days do I get?"   -> [0.9, 0.1]
"Paid leave accrues at 1.5 days/month" -> [0.8, 0.2]
"Submit expense receipts within 30 days" -> [0.1, 0.9]

The first two points sit close together on the map, even though they share almost no words. The third is far away. That is all an embedding is: coordinates for meaning.

A real embedding model (here OpenAI's, producing 1,536 numbers) uses 1,536 such "directions" instead of 2, and it learned them by itself from huge amounts of text, so they don't have simple names. But the idea is the same: similar meaning, nearby points.

To search, you turn the user's question into an embedding too, then ask the database: "which stored chunks point in the most similar direction?". Cosine similarity is the formula that scores how similar two directions are: close to 1 means very similar, close to 0 means unrelated.

New to tool calling? How the model "asks" your app for help

A model cannot run code or query your database by itself. With tool calling, you describe some functions to the model when you send the request, for example: "searchKnowledgeBase: searches the company documents; input: a search query".

If the model decides it needs that information, instead of answering it replies with a structured message meaning "please run searchKnowledgeBase with query 'vacation policy'". The AI SDK runs your function, sends the results back to the model, and the model then writes the final answer. If the user just says "hello", the model can answer directly without searching.

Where does "indexing" fit? Everything that prepares documents for retrieval (chunking, embedding, storing, and the database's vector index) is called indexing. It happens once per upload so that every question is cheap. The full explanation, with diagrams, is in Indexing Explained.

Recap
  • RAG = retrieve relevant passages, augment the model's context with them, generate the answer.
  • The model's knowledge stays current because the documents are looked up when a question is asked.
