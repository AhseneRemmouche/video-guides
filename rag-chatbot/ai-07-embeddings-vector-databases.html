Embeddings & Vector Databases

Embedding models, distance metrics, vector stores and approximate search.

Your chatbot finds the right paragraph of the employee handbook without matching a single keyword. "How much PTO do I get?" finds a chunk that talks about "vacation days". That works because of two things: embeddings, which turn text into lists of numbers that capture meaning, and a vector database, which stores those lists and quickly finds the ones closest to a question.

In this chapter you will learn:

  • what an embedding captures, and just as important, what it doesn't;
  • what an embedding model is, what "1,536 dimensions" means, and how to pick a model;
  • the three common ways to measure "closeness" (cosine, dot product, Euclidean) and why they often give the same answer;
  • which vector databases exist, and how to choose between Postgres + pgvector (used here) and dedicated vector stores;
  • exact vs approximate search, and the two pgvector index types (HNSW and IVFFlat) at a high level;
  • filtering by metadata, multilingual and multimodal embeddings, and the storage and cost maths.

Why this matters for your chatbot. Every answer the bot gives is only as good as the chunks the search returns. If you understand what the vectors in your documents table really represent, you can predict when search will work, notice when it won't, and make good decisions when the knowledge base grows from one PDF to thousands.

This chapter builds on pages you have already seen. It links to them instead of repeating them: step 9 (creating the pgvector table), step 10 (the embedding functions), step 14 (the similarity search) and Indexing Explained (the HNSW index in depth).

Embeddings, a quick recap

An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers (a vector) that a model produces from a piece of input, usually text. In this project, every chunk of every uploaded document becomes a list of 1,536 numbers, and every question becomes one too. Texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions. If you have never seen this idea before, read the 2-number primer in step 10 first; it is the best picture to keep in your head.

The project code that creates them is short. This is from src/lib/embeddings.ts:

const { embedding } = await embed({
  model: openai.textEmbeddingModel("text-embedding-3-small"),
  value: input,
});

embed turns one text into one vector (used for the question). Its sibling embedMany turns an array of texts into an array of vectors in one request (used for all chunks of an upload). Both come from the AI SDK.

What embeddings capture, and what they don't

An embedding model was trained so that texts people would consider "about the same thing" land near each other. That gives it some impressive abilities, and some blind spots that surprise beginners.

Embeddings are good atExample
Synonyms and paraphrases"PTO" ≈ "vacation days" ≈ "time off"
Topic and intent"My laptop won't turn on" lands near IT support text, not near holiday policy
Different phrasing of the same question"Can I work from home?" ≈ "remote work policy"
Some cross-language matching (model-dependent)"congés payés" may land near "paid leave"
Embeddings are weak atWhy it happensWhat it means for you
Exact codes, IDs and rare names (e.g. "form HR-104B", an employee number)The vector stores the gist, not each character. Two different codes look almost identical in meaning.Combine with keyword search (hybrid search, see Advanced RAG).
Numbers and comparisons ("more than 5 years")"15 days" and "20 days" are about the same topic, so they are close.Retrieve the topic, then let the model read the numbers. Use the calculator tool for maths.
Negation ("not eligible" vs "eligible")Both sentences are about eligibility, so they often end up close.Retrieval finds the topic; the model must read carefully.
Truth and freshnessAn embedding knows nothing about whether a text is correct or out of date.Remove old versions of documents (see Advanced RAG).
Very long textsOne vector for a 50-page PDF is a blur of every topic in it.That's why we chunk (step 11).

Common misconception: "Similar vectors mean the chunk answers the question." Not quite. Similar vectors mean the chunk is about the same thing as the question. A chunk saying "Vacation requests must be filed two weeks ahead" is very similar to "How many vacation days do I get?" but doesn't answer it. This is why the bot retrieves 3 chunks, not 1, and why advanced systems add a reranking step.

Embedding models and dimensions

An embedding model is a neural network (see neural network) whose output is a vector instead of text. It is a different model from the chat model: this project uses gpt-5-mini to write answers and text-embedding-3-small to embed text. Embedding models are much cheaper per token than chat models, because they do one pass over the input and never generate text.

New to "dimensions"? What 1,536 actually means

A dimension is one slot in the list. A point on a flat map has 2 dimensions (x, y). A point in a room has 3 (x, y, height). An embedding from text-embedding-3-small has 1,536: it's a point in a space with 1,536 directions.

You can't picture 1,536 directions, and you don't need to. All the maths you use (angles, distances) works the same way as on a 2-D map, just with more numbers to add up.

No single number means anything readable like "is about holidays". The meaning is spread across all of them. That's why you can't "edit" a vector by hand, only compare it with others.

Facts about the model this project uses (check the OpenAI embeddings guide for current details):

  • text-embedding-3-small returns 1,536 numbers by default. That's why step 9 declares vector("embedding", { dimensions: 1536 }) and the migration creates vector(1536).
  • Its larger sibling, text-embedding-3-large, returns 3,072 by default.
  • Both accept a dimensions option that returns a shorter vector (for example 512) in exchange for a little quality. Shorter vectors are cheaper to store and faster to compare.
  • OpenAI's embeddings come back already normalized (length 1). This matters for the distance section below.
  • Each input has a maximum length (roughly 8,000 tokens for these models at the time of writing). A 900-character chunk is far below that.

In the AI SDK, provider-specific options like dimensions go in providerOptions:

import { embed } from "ai";
import { openai } from "@ai-sdk/openai";

const { embedding } = await embed({
  model: openai.textEmbeddingModel("text-embedding-3-small"),
  value: "How many vacation days do I get?",
  providerOptions: { openai: { dimensions: 512 } },
});

console.log(embedding.length); // 512

If you change dimensions, you change the schema. The column type vector(1536) only accepts 1,536 numbers. A 512-number vector would be rejected on insert. Changing the model or its dimensions means: update db-schema.ts, run a new migration, and re-embed every chunk. See "When you must re-index" in Indexing Explained.

Other providers (examples, at the time of writing in 2025)

OpenAI is one of many embedding providers. Examples include Cohere, Google (Gemini embedding models), Voyage AI and Mistral, plus open-weight models you can run yourself as a local model (for example through Ollama or the sentence-transformers library). Their dimensions range from a few hundred to several thousand. The AI SDK has providers for several of them, so swapping is mostly a one-line change to the model, plus the schema change and re-embedding above. Model lists change often; always check each provider's current documentation.

When comparing embedding models, look at these properties rather than at "which one is best":

PropertyWhy it matters
Quality on your dataPublic leaderboards measure general tasks. Your HR handbooks may behave differently. Test with 20–50 real questions (see Evaluating AI Applications).
DimensionsMore dimensions = more storage and slower comparisons. Not automatically better.
LanguagesIf your documents or users aren't English-only, check multilingual support.

The golden rule: vectors from different models (or the same model with different dimensions) cannot be compared. Each model has its own private "map". The chunks and the question must always be embedded by the same model with the same settings. Mixing them produces meaningless scores without any error message.

Distance metrics: how "close" is measured

Search needs a number that says how similar two vectors are. There are three common choices. Picture each vector as an arrow starting at the origin of the map.

MetricIntuitionRange"More similar" meanspgvector operator
Cosine similarityDo the arrows point the same way? Length ignored.−1 to 1 (similarity); 0 to 2 as cosine distance = 1 − similarityHigher similarity / lower distance<=> (cosine distance) ✓ this project
Dot product (inner product)Multiply matching numbers and add them up. Rewards same direction and long arrows.UnboundedHigher<#> (returns the negative inner product, so smaller = more similar)
Euclidean (L2) distanceStraight-line distance between the arrow tips, like a ruler.0 to ∞Lower<->
New to the maths? A worked example with 2 numbers

Take a = [0.6, 0.8] and b = [0.8, 0.6]. Both have length 1 (because 0.6² + 0.8² = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1).

  • Dot product: 0.6×0.8 + 0.8×0.6 = 0.48 + 0.48 = 0.96.
  • Cosine similarity: dot product ÷ (length a × length b) = 0.96 ÷ (1 × 1) = 0.96. So the cosine distance is 1 − 0.96 = 0.04.
  • Euclidean distance: the differences are −0.2 and 0.2, so √(0.04 + 0.04) = √0.08 ≈ 0.283.

Notice that for length-1 vectors, the dot product is the cosine similarity, and squared Euclidean distance = 2 − 2 × cosine similarity (here 2 − 1.92 = 0.08 ✓). That's the maths behind the next section.

When all three agree

If every vector is normalized (scaled to length 1), the three metrics always produce the same ranking. Sorting by highest cosine similarity, highest dot product, or lowest Euclidean distance gives the same top 3. OpenAI's embeddings are normalized, so for this project the choice only affects the numbers you see, not which chunks are returned.

Rules of thumb:

  • Use the metric the embedding model's documentation recommends. For most text models that's cosine.
  • If vectors are normalized, the dot product is slightly cheaper to compute and gives identical rankings, so some systems use it for speed.
  • The metric your index is built with must match the operator your query sorts by. This project builds with vector_cosine_ops and sorts with <=>. See the operator-class table in Indexing Explained, Part 2.

The AI SDK also ships a small helper, cosineSimilarity, handy for experiments in plain TypeScript without a database:

import { cosineSimilarity, embedMany } from "ai";
import { openai } from "@ai-sdk/openai";

const { embeddings } = await embedMany({
  model: openai.textEmbeddingModel("text-embedding-3-small"),
  values: ["How many vacation days do I get?", "PTO allowance per year", "Reset my laptop password"],
});

console.log(cosineSimilarity(embeddings[0], embeddings[1])); // relatively high
console.log(cosineSimilarity(embeddings[0], embeddings[2])); // noticeably lower

Try it with your own sentences. It is the fastest way to build intuition, and to sanity-check the 0.5 threshold used in step 15. Absolute scores differ between models: one model's "0.5" isn't another model's "0.5".

Vector databases: where the vectors live

A vector database stores vectors next to the data they describe and answers one question fast: "which stored vectors are nearest to this one?" (a nearest-neighbour query). There are two broad families.

1. A vector extension for a database you already use

This project uses Postgres with the pgvector extension, hosted on Neon (step 9). pgvector adds a vector column type, distance operators, and two index types. Your vectors sit in the same table as the text, and you query them with normal SQL, so you can join with other tables, filter with WHERE, and use transactions and backups you already know. Other general-purpose databases and search engines offer similar vector features.

2. Dedicated vector stores

These are databases built mainly for vectors. Examples (in alphabetical order, not a ranking) include Chroma, Milvus, Pinecone, Qdrant and Weaviate. Some are managed cloud services, some are open source and self-hostable, some are both. They usually offer their own client SDKs, built-in metadata filtering, and features aimed at very large or very busy vector workloads. Features change quickly, so compare the current documentation of each.

How to choose

Question to askLeans towards Postgres + pgvectorLeans towards a dedicated store
Do you already run Postgres?Yes: one less system to operate, back up and secure.No database yet, or a different stack.
Do vectors need to join with app data (users, orgs, permissions)?Yes: one SQL query can filter and join.Less so, or you're happy to keep IDs in sync between two systems.
How many vectors, how many queries per second?Thousands to millions is common territory.Very large collections or very high query volume may benefit from a purpose-built system. Measure first.
Who operates it?Your existing database host (Neon here).A managed service, or your team self-hosts.
Special features needed?Standard SQL, full-text search built in.Check: built-in hybrid search, multi-tenancy features, specific index types.

This is a rule of thumb, not a law. A sensible default for a new project is: start with the database you already have, measure, and move only when you hit a real limit. For a company-handbook chatbot like this one, pgvector is more than enough.

Exact vs approximate nearest neighbour

There are two ways to find the closest vectors:

  • Exact (k-NN, brute force): compare the question's vector with every stored vector and keep the best k. Always correct. Cost grows in step with the number of rows. This is what Postgres does when there is no usable index.
  • Approximate (ANN): use an index to check only a small, promising part of the data. Much faster at scale. Very occasionally returns the 4th-best match instead of the 3rd-best. How often it gets the true top k is called recall.

The full explanation, including a measured comparison, the phone-book analogy and why recall rarely matters for RAG, is in Indexing Explained, Part 2. One important practical point from there: the tutorial's search in step 14 sorts by desc(similarity), which prevents Postgres from using the index. The recommended version sorts by the raw distance so the index is used.

Because the tutorial's table starts empty and grows with each upload, HNSW is the natural fit. The pgvector README has current recommendations for both index types and their parameters.

-- The project's index (from the repo's migration):
CREATE INDEX "embeddingIndex" ON "documents" USING hnsw ("embedding" vector_cosine_ops);

-- What an IVFFlat index would look like instead (not used here):
CREATE INDEX ON documents USING ivfflat (embedding vector_cosine_ops) WITH (lists = 100);
SET ivfflat.probes = 10;

Metadata filtering

Real apps rarely want "the nearest chunks in the whole database". They want "the nearest chunks that this user may see", or "from the 2025 handbook only". Extra columns that describe each chunk (file name, page, department, organisation, date) are metadata, and filtering on them is metadata filtering.

The current documents table has only id, content and embedding. With an extra column you'd write:

SELECT id, content, 1 - (embedding <=> $1) AS similarity
FROM documents
WHERE department = 'hr'          -- metadata filter
ORDER BY embedding <=> $1       -- raw distance, so the HNSW index can be used
LIMIT 3;

A subtlety worth knowing: an approximate index first finds a fixed number of candidates (for HNSW, controlled by hnsw.ef_search, default 40), and the WHERE filter is applied to those. If the filter is very selective (say only 1% of rows match), you may get fewer than 3 results back. Common remedies:

  • raise hnsw.ef_search for that query;
  • use pgvector's iterative index scans (added in version 0.8.0), which keep searching until enough rows pass the filter;
  • add a normal B-tree index on the filter column, or a partial index per common filter value, so Postgres can choose a better plan;
  • for strict per-customer separation, consider partitioning by customer.

Filtering for security (only show a user their own organisation's documents) is covered in Advanced RAG and Security & Safety.

Multilingual and multimodal embeddings

Multilingual

A multilingual embedding model maps the same meaning in different languages to nearby vectors, so a question in Spanish can find a chunk written in English. Many modern hosted models handle several languages, but quality varies a lot by language and model. If your users ask in one language and your documents are in another, test it with real questions before relying on it. Alternatives are translating documents at indexing time or translating the question before searching.

Multimodal

A multimodal embedding model can embed different kinds of input, typically images and text, into the same space, so the text "a red fire extinguisher" lands near a photo of one. This enables "search images by description" and "find text related to this picture".

This project takes a different, simpler route in step 21: a vision model describes each image or scanned page as text (a form of OCR), and that text is then chunked and embedded with the same text model as everything else. One embedding model, one table, one search. The trade-off: anything the description leaves out can't be found.

Storage and cost maths

pgvector stores each number in a vector column as a 4-byte floating-point value (a "float32"), plus a small fixed header of 8 bytes per vector.

CalculationResult
One vector, 1,536 dims: 1,536 × 4 bytes + 86,152 bytes ≈ 6 KB
The chunk's text: 900 characters (mostly 1 byte each in English)≈ 0.9 KB, so the vector is roughly 7× bigger than the text it describes
10,000 chunks × 6,152 bytes≈ 61.5 MB of vectors
1,000,000 chunks × 6,152 bytes≈ 6.15 GB of vectors (≈ 5.7 GiB)
Same, with 3,072 dims (12,296 bytes each)≈ 12.3 GB
Same, with 512 dims (2,056 bytes each)≈ 2.06 GB

The HNSW index is stored in addition to the table, and is fastest when it fits in memory, so plan for noticeably more than the raw figure. pgvector also offers a halfvec type that stores 2-byte numbers, roughly halving the space, usually with very little effect on search quality. That is a form of quantization. The pgvector README lists the current limits for each type (for example, the maximum number of dimensions an HNSW index supports).

Embedding cost is charged per input token. A rough estimate (rule of thumb: about 4 characters per token for English text):

tokens per chunk     ≈ 900 characters ÷ 4      ≈ 225 tokens
tokens per document  ≈ chunks × 225             (overlap makes this a bit more than the raw text)
cost to index        ≈ total tokens × provider's price per token
cost per question    ≈ question tokens × price  (tiny: one short embed call)

Look up the current price on your provider's pricing page; prices change and are not listed here. Two practical takeaways: indexing is a one-off cost per document version, and re-embedding everything (after changing model) costs that again.

Pitfalls

  • Different models for chunks and questions. Scores become meaningless and nothing errors.
  • Treating similarity as a probability. A similarity of 0.6 doesn't mean "60% relevant". Thresholds must be tuned per model and per dataset.
  • Index built for one metric, query sorted by another. The index silently goes unused.
  • Expecting embeddings to match exact codes or names. Use keyword or hybrid search for those.
  • Changing dimensions without a migration. Inserts fail with a dimension mismatch error.
  • Picking a database for scale you don't have. Start simple and measure.
Recap
  • An embedding is a fixed-length vector capturing the gist of a text. It's great for synonyms and topics, weak for exact codes, numbers, negation and freshness.
  • This project uses text-embedding-3-small, which returns 1,536 numbers; the schema's vector(1536) must match. Never mix vectors from different models.
  • Cosine compares direction, Euclidean compares positions, dot product mixes both. For normalized vectors (like OpenAI's), all three rank results the same.
  • pgvector keeps vectors in Postgres with your other data; dedicated stores (Chroma, Milvus, Pinecone, Qdrant, Weaviate and others) are purpose-built alternatives. Start with what you have.
  • Exact search checks every row; ANN indexes (HNSW, IVFFlat) check a fraction and trade a tiny bit of recall for speed.
  • Metadata filters narrow results, but very selective filters on ANN indexes need care.
  • Each 1,536-dim vector is about 6 KB, roughly 7× the size of a 900-character chunk.
