How Large Language Models Work

Tokens, training, next-token prediction, temperature, context windows, hallucinations and reasoning models.

In chapter 1 you saw that an LLM generates text by predicting what comes next. This chapter opens the box a little further. You will learn what tokens are, how a model is trained in stages, how settings such as temperature change its output, why long conversations cost more, why models make things up, what "reasoning models" do differently, and how all of this shows up in the code you wrote in Part 1.

Why this matters for your chatbot. Almost every practical question about an AI app comes back to these mechanics. "Why did my bill go up?" (tokens and history). "Why did it invent a policy?" (hallucination). "Why is the first word slow to appear?" (reasoning and streaming). "Why doesn't it know about last week's document?" (knowledge cutoff, which is why we built RAG).

Tokens and tokenization

Models do not read letters or whole words. They read tokens: small chunks of text taken from a fixed vocabulary that the model's creators built before training. A token can be a whole common word, part of a word, a space plus a word, a punctuation mark, or a few bytes of an unusual character.

The step that cuts text into tokens is called tokenization, and the program that does it is a tokenizer. Each model family has its own tokenizer, so the exact split differs between models. Here is the kind of split you would see (illustrative, not exact for any specific model):

TextPossible tokensWhy
"The cat sat"The · cat · satCommon words are usually one token each, often including the space before them.
"unbelievably"un · bel · iev · ablyRarer words are split into reusable pieces.
"pgvector"pg · vectorTechnical names are built from smaller known pieces.
"2025-07-14"202 · 5 · - · 07 · - · 14Numbers are often chopped into small groups of digits, one reason models can stumble on arithmetic.

Rule of thumb for English: one token is about 4 characters, or roughly ¾ of a word. So 100 tokens ≈ 75 words, and a 1,000-word document is somewhere around 1,300 tokens. Other languages, code and unusual text often need more tokens per word. Treat this as an estimate; providers offer tokenizer tools for exact counts.

New to tokens? Why they matter so much in practice

Tokens are the unit for almost everything you care about as a developer:

  • Cost: API providers bill per token, usually with separate rates for input tokens (what you send) and output tokens (what the model writes).
  • Limits: every model has a maximum number of tokens it can handle in one request (the context window).
  • Speed: models write one token at a time, so a long answer takes longer than a short one.

The AI SDK reports usage for every call, for example inputTokens and outputTokens in the usage result, so you can log it and watch your costs.

Next-token prediction: the core loop

An LLM does one thing over and over: given all the tokens so far, it outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary (tens of thousands of options) of being the next one. For the text "The capital of France is" it might score Paris very high, a lower, the lower still, and banana near zero.

Then a token is picked from those probabilities (that is sampling, covered below), added to the end of the text, and the whole thing runs again with the slightly longer text. This continues until the model produces a special "end" token or hits a length limit.

Two surprising consequences:

  • The model has no plan written down anywhere. It does not draft the whole answer and then send it. Whatever "planning" happens is implicit in each next-token choice (reasoning models, below, add an explicit thinking phase).
  • Every token costs a full pass through the model. That is why long answers take longer and why output tokens are usually priced higher than input tokens.

How a chat model is made: three training stages

The chat models you call through an API usually go through several stages of training. The exact recipes are proprietary and vary, but the broad shape is well known:

  1. Pretraining. The model learns next-token prediction on an enormous amount of text (web pages, books, code and more). This is self-supervised: the "correct answer" is just the real next token. The result is a base model: very knowledgeable, but it simply continues text. Ask it "What is the capital of France?" and it might continue with more quiz questions instead of answering.
  2. Instruction tuning (supervised fine-tuning). The base model is trained further on many example conversations written or curated by people: an instruction and a good response. Now it learns the format of being an assistant: answer the question, follow the instruction, stop when done.
  3. Preference tuning (RLHF and similar methods). People (and sometimes other models) compare pairs of responses and say which is better: more helpful, more honest, safer. The model is then tuned to produce the preferred kind of answer. RLHF stands for reinforcement learning from human feedback; newer variants exist, but the idea is the same: learn from judgements of quality, not just from examples.

Why you should care: stage 3 is why models are polite, follow system prompts and refuse some requests. It is also part of why they can be over-eager to please, sometimes producing a confident answer rather than admitting uncertainty. Your system prompt in step 15 pushes against that by telling the model to base its answers on the search results.

Inference and sampling: temperature and top-p

At inference time, the model gives probabilities, but something has to choose the next token. Always picking the single most likely token ("greedy" decoding) tends to produce repetitive, flat text. So most systems sample: they pick randomly, weighted by the probabilities. Two common settings control that randomness:

SettingWhat it doesLow valueHigh value
TemperatureSharpens or flattens the probabilities before picking.Near 0: almost always the most likely token. Focused, repeatable, a bit dull.Higher: unlikely tokens get more chance. More varied and creative, and more likely to drift or make mistakes.
Top-p (nucleus sampling)Only considers the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to p, then samples among those.e.g. 0.1: only the very top candidates are allowed.1.0: every token is allowed (no cut-off).

An example of the effect, for the prompt "Write a name for a coffee shop" (illustrative):

  • Low temperature: asking five times gives "The Daily Grind", "The Daily Grind", "The Daily Grind", "Bean There", "The Daily Grind".
  • High temperature: asking five times gives "Moonlit Roastery", "The Velvet Bean", "Grounds for Celebration", "Steam & Stories", "Copper Kettle Collective".

Rule of thumb: use low temperature for factual Q&A, extraction and classification; use moderate-to-higher temperature for brainstorming and creative writing. A common recommendation is to change temperature or top-p, not both.

In the AI SDK both are plain options:

import { generateText } from "ai";
import { openai } from "@ai-sdk/openai";

const { text, usage } = await generateText({
  model: openai("gpt-4.1-mini"),
  prompt: "Write a name for a coffee shop.",
  temperature: 1.2,
  maxOutputTokens: 50,
});

console.log(text, usage.inputTokens, usage.outputTokens);

The context window, and why history costs tokens

The context window is the maximum number of tokens a model can consider in one request: your system prompt, the whole conversation, any documents or tool results, and the answer it writes. Context sizes vary a lot between models, from a few thousand tokens in older or small models to hundreds of thousands or more in some current ones. Check each model's documentation.

Here is the crucial point beginners miss: the model has no memory between requests. It is stateless. A conversation only "continues" because your app sends the entire history every time.

Look at the chat page from step 6:

const { messages, sendMessage, status } = useChat();

When the user types a new question, sendMessage adds it to messages and, by default, useChat POSTs the whole messages array to /api/chat. The route from step 15 then forwards all of it to the model:

const { messages }: { messages: ChatMessage[] } = await req.json();

const result = streamText({
  model: openai("gpt-5-mini"),
  messages: convertToModelMessages(messages),

So turn 1 sends one message, turn 2 sends three (question, answer, new question), turn 10 sends nineteen, plus any tool results stored in earlier messages. Each request pays for all of those input tokens again.

Pitfalls

  • Bills that grow with conversation length. Long chats get more expensive per message. Common fixes: only send the last N messages, summarise old turns, or drop bulky tool results from history. Chapter 14 covers cost control.
  • "Context length exceeded" errors. If history plus retrieved chunks plus the answer would exceed the window, the request fails. Trim history or retrieve fewer chunks.
  • Lost in the middle. Even inside the window, models do not use every token equally well; information buried in a very long prompt can be overlooked. Fewer, more relevant chunks often beat more chunks.

Knowledge cutoff, and why RAG exists

Pretraining uses data collected up to some date. After that, the model learns nothing new; that date is its knowledge cutoff. The model also never saw your private data: your company handbook, your PDFs, your database.

You could, in theory, retrain or fine-tune the model on your documents, but that is slow, costly, hard to update and still does not guarantee exact recall. RAG takes a simpler route: look up the relevant passages at question time and put them in the prompt. That is exactly the searchKnowledgeBase tool from step 15. New document uploaded a minute ago? It is searchable immediately, no training required. Chapter 15 compares prompting, RAG and fine-tuning in detail.

Hallucinations: why models make things up

A hallucination is a fluent, confident answer that is false: an invented citation, a made-up API function, a policy your company never had.

Why does it happen? Remember the core loop: the model always produces some next token, choosing what is plausible given its training. It has no built-in fact-checker and no reliable sense of "I have never seen this". If the true answer is not in its parameters or its prompt, a plausible-sounding guess is often what comes out. Preference training can make this worse if confident answers were rewarded over honest "I don't know"s.

You cannot eliminate hallucinations, but you can reduce them a lot:

  • Ground the model with RAG. Put the real text in the prompt and tell it to answer from that. Your system prompt does this: "Base your answers on the search results when available."
  • Give it tools for things it is bad at. The calculator tool in step 22 means arithmetic is computed, not guessed.
  • Allow "I don't know". Explicitly say: "If the search results do not contain the answer, say so." Models follow this surprisingly well.
  • Ask for sources. Have it cite which chunk it used, so users (and you) can check (chapter 8).
  • Lower temperature for factual tasks, and evaluate regularly (chapter 12).

Reasoning models: thinking before answering

A reasoning model is trained to work through a problem step by step in an internal "thinking" phase before writing its visible answer. Those thinking steps are also tokens; they are generated by the same next-token loop, but they are usually hidden from you (some providers return a summary of them). The model has been trained, often with reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers, to use that thinking productively.

BenefitCost
Better at multi-step problems: maths, logic, planning, tricky code, deciding which tool to call.Slower: the first visible word can take noticeably longer, because thinking happens first.
Often more careful with instructions and edge cases.More tokens: reasoning tokens are typically billed as output tokens even though you do not see them.
Some providers let you choose how much effort it spends (for example a "reasoning effort" option).Some classic sampling settings such as temperature may not be supported; check the provider docs. The AI SDK reports unsupported settings as warnings.

In this project: the repo uses gpt-5-mini, which OpenAI documents as part of its GPT-5 family of reasoning models; the video uses gpt-4.1-mini, a non-reasoning model. Both work for the chatbot. You may notice gpt-5-mini pauses a little longer before the answer starts streaming; that is the thinking phase. As noted in step 5, gpt-5-mini also needs an extra setting if your OpenAI organisation uses zero data retention. Model capabilities change over time, so check OpenAI's model pages for current details.

Rule of thumb: use a reasoning model when the task needs several steps of thought (an agent choosing tools, analysing a contract). Use a fast non-reasoning model for simple, latency-sensitive work (rewriting, classification, OCR).

System, user and assistant roles

Chat models are trained on conversations with labelled turns. Each message has a role:

  • system: instructions from you, the developer about how the assistant should behave. Users normally cannot see it. In the AI SDK you pass it as the system option, as in step 15: "You are a helpful assistant with access to a knowledge base…"
  • user: what the person typed (or said, after transcription, or uploaded as an image).
  • assistant: what the model replied earlier, including any tool calls it made.
  • tool: the results your code returned from a tool call, fed back to the model.
const { text } = await generateText({
  model: openai("gpt-4.1-mini"),
  system: "You answer in one short sentence.",
  messages: [
    { role: "user", content: "What is pgvector?" },
    { role: "assistant", content: "A Postgres extension for storing and searching vectors." },
    { role: "user", content: "Who would use it?" },
  ],
});

Streaming tokens

Because the model produces one token at a time, an app can show each token as soon as it exists instead of waiting for the whole answer. That is streaming. It does not make the model faster, but it makes the app feel much faster: the user starts reading after a fraction of a second instead of staring at a spinner.

In Part 1, streamText starts the generation loop on OpenAI's servers, result.toUIMessageStreamResponse() forwards the tokens (and tool-call events) to the browser as an HTTP stream, and useChat updates messages as pieces arrive, so React re-renders the growing answer. The status value moves from "submitted" (waiting for the first token) to "streaming" (tokens arriving) to "ready".

Two speed numbers are worth knowing: time to first token (how long until anything appears; reasoning and long prompts increase it) and tokens per second (how fast text flows once it starts). Latency is covered in chapter 14.

Common misconceptions

  • "The model learns from my chats." Not during your request. Weights are frozen at inference. (Whether a provider may use API data for future training is a policy question; check their data-usage terms.)
  • "Bigger context window means I can skip RAG." Stuffing everything in costs more per request, is slower, and can reduce answer quality. Retrieval is still useful.
  • "Temperature 0 makes it fully deterministic." It makes output much more consistent, but providers generally do not guarantee identical results every time.
Recap
  • Models read and write tokens (≈ ¾ of an English word). Tokens drive cost, limits and speed.
  • Generation is a loop: score every next token, sample one, append, repeat.
  • Chat models are made by pretraining, instruction tuning and preference tuning (RLHF).
  • Temperature and top-p control randomness: low for facts, higher for creativity.
  • Models are stateless; useChat resends the whole history, so long chats cost more tokens.
  • Knowledge cutoffs and private data are why we use RAG; RAG and tools also reduce hallucinations.
  • Reasoning models (such as the GPT-5 family, including gpt-5-mini) think before answering: smarter on hard tasks, slower and more token-hungry.
  • Embedding models output vectors, chat models output text; messages have system, user, assistant and tool roles; streaming shows tokens as they are generated.
