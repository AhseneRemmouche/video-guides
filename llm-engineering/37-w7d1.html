Week 7 · Day 1 — QLoRA and Loading the Base Model

The idea behind QLoRA: freeze a 4-bit quantized open-source model and train tiny low-rank adapters on top. Load Llama in 4-bit and prepare the pricing dataset.

Welcome to Week 7, the week the instructor calls a "massive" and advanced one. Last week you fine-tuned a frontier model and the result was a slight disappointment: a huge, already-excellent model had little room to improve on a task it already handled. This week flips the approach. You take a far smaller open-source model — Meta's Llama 3.1 8B — and train it on your own pricing data. A smaller model has much more room to grow, and the technique that makes this cheap enough to run on a single budget GPU is called QLoRA.

Today is the setup day. You will learn the ideas behind QLoRA, then load the Llama 3.1 8B base model squeezed down to 4-bit so it fits in memory, load the pricing dataset you built back in Week 6, and see how one training example is shaped. No training happens yet — that is Days 3 and 4. First, the big idea.

The big idea: freeze the giant, train a tiny add-on

Llama 3.1 comes in three sizes: 8 billion, 70 billion, and a monstrous 405 billion parameters. Even the smallest, the 8B, is too big to train the normal way. Its 8 billion weights take about 32 GB of memory just to hold the model — and real training needs several times that, because you also store a gradient for every single weight. A cheap single-GPU box (the free Colab T4 has only about 15 GB) cannot come close. Training the biggest models from scratch has cost labs over $100 million. That is not our budget.

QLoRA is the stack of tricks that makes this affordable. It has two halves, and the name is literally the two letters joined: Q for quantization and LoRA for the low-rank adapters.

  1. Shrink Quantize the base model to 4-bit so the whole thing fits in a small GPU's memory.
  2. Freeze Freeze every one of those 8 billion weights — we will not change them at all.
  3. Add Attach small low-rank adapter matrices onto a few chosen layers, and train only those.

The result: instead of adjusting 8 billion weights, you adjust about 27 million — roughly 100 MB of new numbers riding on top of a frozen 32 GB model. That is why it is cheap, and it works surprisingly well.

New to fine-tuning? A quick recap of what it means

Fine-tuning is just another word for training — but you never start from nothing. Training a model from scratch costs a fortune, so instead you take a model that is already trained (a base or pre-trained model) and train it a little more on your own examples so it gets better at one specific task. Last week you did this to a frontier model through OpenAI's API, where the training ran on OpenAI's servers and you never saw the weights. This week is different: the model is open-source, so the weights are on your GPU, and you run the training. That is what makes techniques like LoRA and quantization matter — they are about fitting the job onto hardware you can actually afford.

New to LoRA and low-rank adapters? What they are and why they matter

LoRA stands for Low-Rank Adaptation. The idea: don't touch the model's own weights at all. Pick a few layers you care about (the instructor calls these the target modules), and for each one add two small new matrices off to the side, named lora_A and lora_B in the code. These are much smaller — "lower rank" means fewer dimensions, so far fewer numbers. During training only these small matrices change. When the model runs, the two matrices are multiplied together, scaled, and added as a small nudge on top of the frozen layer. Because the matrices are tiny, there is very little to train, which is why it is fast and cheap. Those small side matrices are the adapters.

New to "freezing" a model? What it means and why it matters

Normal training works by optimization: the model makes a prediction, you compare it to the right answer, and then you nudge every weight a little so next time the prediction is slightly better. Doing that for 8 billion weights is what is too expensive. Freezing a weight simply means "leave this one alone" — no gradient is computed for it and it never changes during training. In LoRA you freeze all of the base model's weights, so none of the heavy machinery runs for them. Only the small adapter matrices are left unfrozen, so only they get trained. Freezing is what turns an 8-billion-weight training problem into a 27-million-weight one.

The Q in QLoRA: quantization

LoRA solves the training cost, but there is still a problem: even loading the 8B base model takes about 32 GB, and the cheap T4 GPU has only 15 GB. The model will not even fit before you start. Quantization is the fix. Rather than cut the number of weights (which would lose the model's power), you cut the precision of each weight. Normally each weight is a 32-bit floating-point number — a very finely graded dial. Quantizing replaces it with a coarser one: 8-bit, or even 4-bit, which is like a dial with only 16 settings.

The surprising discovery is that this barely hurts the model. You keep all 8 billion weights, just store each one in less space. Llama 3.1 8B drops from 32 GB (32-bit) to about 9 GB (8-bit) to about 5.6 GB (4-bit with the double-quant trick) — and now it fits comfortably on the 15 GB T4. One important detail: quantization shrinks the base model only. The LoRA adapters stay at full 32-bit precision, because they are small and we want them to train accurately.

New to QLoRA = quantization + LoRA? How the two halves fit together

QLoRA is just the two tricks used together. Quantization shrinks the base model so it fits in the GPU's memory. LoRA means you train only small adapters instead of the whole model. One solves the "it doesn't fit" problem, the other solves the "it's too expensive to train" problem. Put them together and you can fine-tune an 8-billion-parameter model on a single cheap GPU: load the base model in 4-bit, freeze it, and train 32-bit LoRA adapters on top. That combination is the whole point of this week.

New to PEFT? What the library is and why it matters

PEFT stands for Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning — the family of techniques, LoRA among them, that fine-tune a model by training only a small number of extra parameters instead of all of them. Hugging Face ships a library of the same name, peft, that implements these methods for you. You will install it this week and use it on Days 3 and 4 to wrap the frozen base model with LoRA adapters. Today's file only loads and quantizes the base model, so peft appears in the week's installs rather than in this first script — but it is the library that makes the "train only the adapters" idea real in code.

Three hyperparameters you will meet

When you configure the adapters on Days 3 and 4, three settings matter most. They are worth meeting now, because the instructor introduces them today. A hyperparameter is simply a knob you choose yourself; there is no formula for the right value, so you try a few and keep what works.

NameWhat it isRule of thumb
r (rank)How many dimensions the low-rank adapter matrices have. Smaller = less memory, faster; larger = more capacity.Start at 8, then double to 16, 32… until more stops helping. The instructor settled on 32.
alphaA scaling factor applied to the adapters. The adjustment added to a layer is alpha × A × B, so bigger alpha means a bigger effect.Set it to double r. So r=32 gives alpha=64.
target modulesWhich layers get adapters. For Llama these are the attention projections q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj.Targeting the attention layers is by far the most common choice, and the one used here.

What today's script does

With the theory in place, the setup script is short. It logs in to Hugging Face, loads the Week 6 pricing dataset, prepares the tokenizer, builds a 4-bit quantization config, and loads the quantized base model. At the end you have the frozen 4-bit Llama in memory, ready for the LoRA adapters on Days 3 and 4.

Before you run it: a gated model and a GPU
  • Llama is gated. Meta's Llama weights require you to accept a license on the model's Hugging Face page and to log in with a Hugging Face token. Without that, the download is refused. In Colab the token is stored as a secret named HF_TOKEN; the script reads it with userdata.get("HF_TOKEN").
  • You need a GPU. This runs on Colab (or any machine with a suitable GPU). On the free T4, pick a GPU runtime before you start.
  • Out of memory without 4-bit. Loading the model at full precision needs about 32 GB and will not fit the 15 GB T4 — you get an out-of-memory error, or it spills onto the CPU and crawls. Keep QUANT_4_BIT = True so the model loads in 4-bit (~5.6 GB).

# Reconstructed to match the course's Google Colab notebook for this day.
# Standard QLoRA/PEFT code; runs on a GPU (e.g. Colab). Not executed here (no GPU).
#
# Week 7 - Day 1 / Day 2: pick the open-source base model (Llama 3.1 8B), load it
# quantized to 4-bit with BitsAndBytes (QLoRA), load the pricing dataset from the
# HuggingFace Hub (built back in Week 6), and show how a training prompt is built.
#
# The big idea (QLoRA): we do NOT fine-tune the 8B base weights. We freeze the
# quantized base model and later (day 3/4) train small low-rank LoRA adapter
# matrices that are applied on top of a few target modules (the attention layers).

import os

import torch
from google.colab import userdata  # on Colab; for local runs read os.environ instead
from huggingface_hub import login
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, BitsAndBytesConfig


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Constants
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The base model. Llama 3.1 comes in 8B / 70B / 405B; the 8B is the only one that
# realistically fits on a single affordable GPU box once quantized.
# We use the plain *base* (foundation) model, NOT the "-Instruct" variant, because
# we train it for one fixed task with one fixed prompt structure.
BASE_MODEL = "meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3.1-8B"

# The pricing dataset built in Week 6 and pushed to the HuggingFace Hub.
# Each row has a "text" field (the prompt, ending in "Price is $NNN") and a
# numeric "price" field. Swap in your own username if you uploaded your own copy.
HF_USER = "ed-donner"
DATASET_NAME = f"{HF_USER}/pricer-data"

# Every training "text" was curated in Week 6 to be at most ~179 tokens. We leave a
# little headroom (beginning-of-sequence + any padding/eos tokens) so the all-important
# final price token is never truncated.
MAX_SEQUENCE_LENGTH = 182

# Quantization choice: True -> 4-bit (fits the ~15GB T4), False -> 8-bit.
QUANT_4_BIT = True


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Log in to the HuggingFace Hub (needed for the gated Llama weights + the dataset)
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

hf_token = userdata.get("HF_TOKEN")  # set this as a Colab secret
login(hf_token, add_to_git_credential=True)


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Load the pricing dataset
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

dataset = load_dataset(DATASET_NAME)
train = dataset["train"]
test = dataset["test"]

print(f"Training items: {len(train):,}")
print(f"Test items: {len(test):,}")

# A training data point: the prompt with the price baked in at the end.
# Example "text":
#   How much does this cost to the nearest dollar?
#
#   <product title and description, truncated to ~179 tokens>
#
#   Price is $226.00
# and a separate numeric field: price = 226.0
print("\nFirst TRAINING item text:\n")
print(train[0]["text"])
print("\nprice field:", train[0]["price"])

# A test data point looks identical EXCEPT the price is withheld - the text stops at
# "Price is $" and it is the model's job to predict the next token (the price).
print("\nFirst TEST item text:\n")
print(test[0]["text"])
print("\n(true price, used only for scoring):", test[0]["price"])


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Tokenizer
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

# Llama 3.1 has a convenient property for this task: every integer 0..999 maps to a
# SINGLE token, so predicting a 3-digit price becomes "predict one token correctly".
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(BASE_MODEL, trust_remote_code=True)
tokenizer.pad_token = tokenizer.eos_token
tokenizer.padding_side = "right"

# Quick sanity check on the one-token-per-price property.
for number in ["0", "1", "10", "100", "999", "1000"]:
    tokens = tokenizer.encode(number, add_special_tokens=False)
    print(f"{number:>5} -> {tokens}")


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4-bit (QLoRA) quantization config via BitsAndBytes
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
#
# nf4           : interpret each 4-bit value on a normal (gaussian) distribution
# double_quant  : a second quantization pass over the quantization constants
#                 (~10-20% extra memory savings, almost free in quality)
# compute_dtype : bfloat16 during the forward/backward math (faster, tiny quality cost)
#
# 8-bit alternative is shown for reference; 4-bit gets the model down to ~5.6GB vs
# ~9GB (8-bit) vs ~32GB (full fp32).

if QUANT_4_BIT:
    quant_config = BitsAndBytesConfig(
        load_in_4bit=True,
        bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,
        bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
        bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",
    )
else:
    quant_config = BitsAndBytesConfig(
        load_in_8bit=True,
        bnb_8bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
    )


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Load the quantized base model
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------

base_model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    BASE_MODEL,
    quantization_config=quant_config,
    device_map="auto",
)
base_model.generation_config.pad_token_id = tokenizer.pad_token_id

# ~5,591.5 MB for the 4-bit double-quant version of Llama 3.1 8B.
print(f"\nMemory footprint: {base_model.get_memory_footprint() / 1e6:,.1f} MB")

# Printing the model shows the architecture: embedding -> 32 x LlamaDecoderLayer
# (each with self-attn q_proj/k_proj/v_proj/o_proj, an MLP, SiLU activation and
# RMSNorm layers) -> final RMSNorm -> lm_head. The attention projections are the
# modules we will target with LoRA adapters in day 3/4.
print(base_model)

Recap
  • Fine-tuning an open-source model yourself needs the model on your own GPU, which is why cost-saving tricks matter this week.
  • QLoRA = Quantization + LoRA: quantization makes the base model fit, LoRA makes it cheap to train.
  • LoRA freezes all 8 billion base weights and trains small low-rank adapter matrices (lora_A/lora_B) on the attention target modules — about 27 million weights, ~100 MB, instead of 8 billion.
  • Quantization lowers each weight's precision, not the number of weights: Llama 3.1 8B goes 32 GB → ~9 GB (8-bit) → ~5.6 GB (4-bit), fitting the 15 GB T4. Only the base model is quantized; the adapters stay full precision.
  • The 4-bit config via bitsandbytes uses nf4, double-quant, and a bfloat16 compute type; the Hugging Face PEFT library applies the adapters on Days 3 and 4.
  • Today's script logs in, loads the base model in 4-bit, loads the pricing dataset, and prepares the tokenizer — nothing is trained yet.
  • Llama gates its weights (license + Hugging Face token) and the full model OOMs without 4-bit; keep QUANT_4_BIT = True. This code is reconstructed to match the Colab lab and was not run here (no GPU).
