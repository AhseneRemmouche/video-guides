Week 6 · Day 3 — Traditional ML Baselines

Before any LLM: feature engineering, a bag-of-words count vectorizer, and classic models (linear regression, SVR, random forest) as baselines to beat.

Companion to Ed Donner's course · uv setup · Python + TypeScript/React · code verified against the repo

Tomorrow this course finally points a large language model at our problem: predict the price of an Amazon product from its text description. But before we do that, we stop and build something deliberately old-fashioned. We build a handful of traditional machine-learning models — the kind data scientists used for decades before deep learning — and measure how well they do. These simple models give us a number to beat. If a fancy LLM cannot do better than a plain word-counting model from the 1990s, then the LLM is not the right tool for this job.

This is a one-day detour into classic machine learning. The instructor cheerfully admits you will either love it or hate it. Either way, the lesson is the same: start simple, measure, then reach for the heavy machinery only once you know what "good" looks like.

Why build a baseline first?

A baseline model is the simplest thing that could possibly work, built first, on purpose. It matters for two reasons the instructor stresses:

  • It is a yardstick. Without a cheap, simple model to compare against, you cannot tell whether your sophisticated model is doing something impressive or just shuffling numbers around. The baseline turns "the model got an answer" into "the model beat the simple approach by this much."
  • The fancy tool is not always the right one. Turning a product description into a number feels like a classic machine-learning task, not obviously an LLM task. Building a baseline keeps us honest: maybe the frontier models will not beat it at all.
New to the idea of a baseline? What it is and why it matters

A baseline is a reference point. Imagine you invent a clever way to guess someone's height from their shoe size. Before celebrating, you should check: how well would you do by just guessing the average height every time? If your clever method is barely better than "always guess average," it is not clever at all. In machine learning the baseline is usually the cheapest, most obvious model you can build quickly. Every later model is reported as "this much better than the baseline," which is far more meaningful than a raw score on its own.

The plan for today

We build a ladder of models, each a little less silly than the last, all using scikit-learn, the standard Python machine-learning library:

  1. Random — ignore the product, return a random number. A deliberately terrible floor.
  2. Constant — always guess the average training price. Surprisingly, much better than random.
  3. Features + linear regression — hand-pick a few properties of the product (its weight, how long its description is…) and fit a straight-line model.
  4. Bag of words + linear regression — stop hand-picking; just count words and let the model decide which words matter.
  5. Random forest — a more powerful model fed the same word counts.

Each model is wrapped in a small pricer function that takes one product and returns an estimated price. We then hand that function to a tester that runs it over 250 held-out products and reports the average error.

Feature engineering: guessing by hand

Feature engineering is the oldest trick in the book. You, the human, decide which measurable properties of a thing probably affect the answer, and you extract those into numbers. For an Amazon product the instructor considers the weight, the Amazon best-seller rank, the length of the description, and whether the brand is a well-known electronics maker.

New to feature engineering? What it is and why it matters

Suppose you want to predict a used car's price. You cannot feed the model "a photo of the car" — so you pick measurable facts: its age, mileage, engine size, number of previous owners. Each of those is a feature. Choosing good features used to be the central skill of a data scientist, and it demanded real knowledge of the subject: you had to know which facts matter. The big surprise of deep learning is that modern models figure out the useful features by themselves, from the raw data — which is why hand feature engineering feels old-fashioned today. It is still worth understanding, because it shows you exactly what the model is "seeing."

Bag of words and the CountVectorizer

Instead of hand-picking features, bag of words lets the text speak for itself. You take a document, count how many times each word appears, and throw away the order entirely. "Red laptop case" and "case laptop red" produce the identical bag. Each document becomes a long list of counts — one slot per word in the vocabulary.

scikit-learn does the counting with a CountVectorizer. Despite the impressive name, it simply learns a vocabulary of the most common words and then counts them. In this notebook it keeps the 2,000 most common words and drops English stop words — the "the", "and", "is" that appear everywhere and carry no signal.

New to bag of words / CountVectorizer? What they are

A computer cannot do arithmetic on the word "laptop" — it needs numbers. Bag of words is the simplest way to get them: list every word you care about (the vocabulary), then describe each document by how many times each of those words appears in it. The result is a row of numbers, mostly zeros, with a count wherever a vocabulary word shows up. CountVectorizer is the scikit-learn tool that builds the vocabulary and fills in the counts for you. It is crude — it ignores word order and meaning — but it is fast and often a strong baseline.

The regressors

Once the text is numbers, we need a model that maps those numbers to a price. The course tries three classic scikit-learn regressors.

Linear regression is the simplest: it assumes the price is a weighted sum of the inputs, and learns the best weight for each one. A random forest is more powerful: it grows many small decision trees, each on a random slice of the data and features, then averages their answers. Support vector regression (SVR) is a third family that tries to fit the data within a tolerance band using "support vectors."

New to linear regression? What it is

Linear regression draws the best straight line (or flat plane, in many dimensions) through your data. If you plotted house size against price and drew the line that best fits the dots, that is linear regression. The model learns one number per feature — its coefficient — saying how strongly that feature pushes the answer up or down. It is fast, easy to interpret, and a classic first model, but it can only capture straight-line relationships.

New to random forests and SVR? What they are

A decision tree asks a chain of yes/no questions ("is the weight over 5 lb? is the word 'Intel' present?") to arrive at a guess. A single tree is shaky, so a random forest builds hundreds of them, each on a random sample of the data and features, and averages the results — an "ensemble." The averaging cancels out the mistakes of any one tree, so forests are robust and need little tuning. Support vector regression (SVR) comes from support vector machines: instead of a line through the points, it tries to fit a tube around them, caring only about the points near the edge (the "support vectors"). The course uses a fast linear SVR; other kinds are far slower.

How the models are scored: average error

The harness runs each pricer over 250 held-out test products. For each one it compares the model's guess to the true price, and the headline number is the plain average error in dollars — how far off the model is, on average. It also colours each result green (close), orange (middling), or red (badly wrong), and plots every guess against the true price so you can see the pattern at a glance.

As you run each pricer through evaluate, the average-dollars number should fall: hundreds for random, around $146 for the constant, down into the $100s once bag of words and the random forest arrive. The scatter plot should show dots creeping closer to the diagonal "perfect guess" line and more of them turning green as the models improve.

Common mistakes and limits
  • Bag of words throws away word order. "Not waterproof" and "waterproof" look almost identical to it. That is the price of its simplicity — it counts words, it does not understand them.
  • Fitting on the training prompt leaks the answer. The code deliberately vectorizes item.summary, not the full training prompt, because the training prompt contains the price. Train on text that includes the answer and the model will look brilliant in training and fail completely on real data (overfitting in its purest form).
  • Use transform, not fit_transform, at prediction time. Each pricer calls vectorizer.transform([…]) so it reuses the vocabulary learned from the training set. Calling fit_transform again would relearn a different vocabulary and break the alignment with the trained model.
  • A random forest can overfit too if it memorises the training set, which is why we judge every model on held-out test products it never saw during fitting.
