Week 4 · Day 1 — How to Choose the Right LLM

The things that actually matter when picking a model: open vs closed, the Chinchilla scaling law, and why benchmarks can mislead (overfitting and training-data leakage).

Companion to Ed Donner's course · uv setup · Python + TypeScript/React · code verified against the repo

Welcome to week four. The instructor opens with a problem every builder runs into: we are spoilt for choice. There are a huge number of large language models out there, split into two camps, and inside each camp there are many more. The question this day answers is simple to state and hard to do well: how do you pick the right model for the task in front of you? This is a conceptual day, so there is no notebook and no code — just the way of thinking an engineer uses to narrow the field.

The most important takeaway, repeated more than once in the video: there is no single best model. It is not a ranking from best to worst. A model is only “better” relative to what you are trying to accomplish. So choosing well means writing down your requirements and weighing each candidate against them.

A two-stage way to narrow the field

The instructor describes a funnel with two stages. First you look at the basics — plain facts about each model, like cost and size. These alone cut a long list down to a short one. Only then do you look at the detailed results — benchmarks, leaderboards and arenas — to separate the finalists. You end up with a handful of candidate models, which you then prototype with before making the final choice.

Stage one: the basics

These are the obvious facts you note down for every model on your shortlist. The first and biggest one is whether the model is open source or closed source, because that choice colours almost everything else. A frontier model like GPT-4o is closed and reached through a paid service; an open-source model like Llama you can download and run yourself.

Here is what each fact tells you, in the order the instructor walks through them:

  • Open or closed source — the first thing to note, because it affects most of the others.
  • Release date and knowledge cutoff — the cutoff is the last date in the training data, beyond which the model usually knows nothing about current events. It may or may not matter for your use case.
  • Number of parameters — the size of the model (in billions of weights). It hints at the model's strength, at its cost, and at how much data you would need to fine-tune it.
  • Number of training tokens — the size of the training dataset, another hint at the model's depth of expertise.
  • Context length — the size of the context window: the total tokens the model can hold at once, which must include the system prompt, the input, and every exchange so far in a chat. If you do multi-shot prompting, all your examples must fit too. In the video, the instructor names Gemini 1.5 Flash, with a one-million-token window, as the longest at the time of recording.

Then come the costs, which the instructor splits into three kinds:

  • Inference cost — what it costs every time you run the model on an input in production. For frontier models this is the API cost (counted in input and output tokens); for a Pro chat interface it is a monthly subscription; for an open-source model you host yourself it is runtime compute (a Colab bill, or a GPU box on a platform like Modal). An open model you run yourself often has a lower cost per call, but the instructor is careful to say this is “not a clear calculus” — it depends on your model and your use case.
  • Training cost — zero for a frontier model used out of the box, but real if you fine-tune an open model for your domain (the subject of week 7).
  • Build cost and time to market — how much work and how long before you are up and running. A big selling point of frontier models is that both are very low; fine-tuning your own open model takes longer and is harder.

The instructor rounds out the basics with a few more:

  • Rate limits and reliability — frontier APIs can cap how often you call them (common on subscription plans), and can return “overloaded” errors when they are too busy. He has seen this with both GPT-4 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet.
  • Speed versus latency — two different things. Speed is throughput, how fast the whole response is generated. Latency is how long before the first token appears. He recalls the airline voice assistant from an earlier week, where latency caused awkward pauses, especially while waiting for images. With your own open model you have more control over this.
  • License — read it, whether the model is open or closed. Many open models have very permissive licenses, but some have fine print: Stable Diffusion allows commercial use only up to a revenue level, above which a business arrangement is needed, and using Llama 3.1 meant signing Meta's terms of service.
New to this? Open source vs closed source, in plain terms

A closed-source (or frontier) model lives behind a company's servers. You cannot download it; you rent access through a paid API or a subscription, and the company controls the model, its updates, and its rate limits. A open-source model is one whose weights you can download and run on your own machine or cloud box. You get more control — over speed, over cost per call, over fine-tuning it on your own data — but you also take on the work of hosting and, if you specialise it, training. Neither is “better”; the instructor's whole point is that the right choice depends on your task.

What good looks like

By the end of the basics you should have a short list: a few models whose cost, size, context window and license actually fit your task. You have not tested them yet — you have just thrown out the ones that could never work, so you do not waste time benchmarking them.

A quick aside: the Chinchilla scaling law

Before the detailed metrics, the instructor pauses on a rule of thumb that connects two of the basics — parameters and training data. The Chinchilla scaling law was coined by Google DeepMind and named after their model, Chinchilla. It says the number of parameters a model needs is roughly proportional to the number of training tokens it is fed.

The intuition the video gives: imagine an 8-billion-parameter model that has reached the point of diminishing returns — adding more training data barely helps any more. That is a good match of data to model size. Now, if you doubled the training data, how many more parameters would you need to actually learn from it? Roughly double — you would go from 8 billion to 16 billion. And it works the other way too: if you move up to a 16-billion-parameter model, you would need to roughly double your training data to take advantage of all those extra weights. The law has stood the test of time for the transformer architecture, and it is a handy rule to keep to hand.

Primer: what is the Chinchilla scaling law for?

It is a planning rule, not a law of physics. It helps you answer two practical questions. “I want a bigger, more capable model — how much more data will it need?” Roughly, in proportion to the extra parameters. “I have this much data — how big a model is worth training?” Again, in proportion. It stops you from building a huge model you cannot feed, or feeding a small model more data than it can absorb. The name comes from DeepMind's Chinchilla model, which demonstrated the balance.

Stage two: benchmarks

Once the basics have given you a short list, you look at benchmarks: standard tests that different models are run through, so their scores can be compared on leaderboards. The instructor stresses you do not need to memorise them — you can always look them up — but it helps to recognise the names. He walks through seven common ones, plus three aimed at more specialised skills.

New to the idea? What a benchmark actually is

A benchmark is a fixed set of questions or tasks with known correct answers. You run a model through the set and record how many it gets right — that score is its benchmark result. Because every model takes the same test, the scores can be lined up on a leaderboard. Benchmarks are how the field talks about “how good” a model is at a skill like maths, reasoning or coding. The catch, which the next section is all about, is that a single number hides a lot.

Why benchmarks can mislead

The instructor calls this one of the most important points of the day, and says it cannot be stressed enough. Benchmarks are useful for comparing where models shine, but they come with real problems you must keep in mind:

  • Not consistently applied. There is no gold standard for how a benchmark is run. A number in a company press release depends on their hardware and their method, so third-party figures should be taken with a pinch of salt.
  • Too narrow in scope. Multiple-choice and very specific answer formats make it hard to measure nuanced reasoning.
  • Training-data leakage. It is very hard to be sure none of the answers appear in the model's training data — and as models train on more recent data, which includes writing about the benchmarks themselves, this gets harder to control.
  • Overfitting. A team can tune a model's many settings over and over until it crushes a specific benchmark, almost by coincidence — and then it fails on a question that tests the same skill but is phrased differently.
  • Evaluation awareness. A newer, still-speculative concern: the strongest frontier models (GPT-4, Claude 3.5 Sonnet level) may sometimes sense they are being tested, which could distort answers — a real worry for safety and alignment questions, though not yet proven.
Primer: what is training-data leakage (contamination)?

Training-data leakage — also called contamination — happens when the benchmark's questions and answers end up inside the data the model was trained on. The model then scores well not because it reasoned the answer out, but because it effectively saw the answer during training, the way a student would ace an exam after being handed the answer key. Because models are trained by scraping enormous amounts of recent text from the internet — including pages that discuss the benchmarks — it is very hard to guarantee the test was never seen. A high score from a “leaked” test tells you little about real skill.

Do not pick a model on a leaderboard alone

A single benchmark number — or a league-table position built from them — is a starting point, not an answer. Because of leakage and overfitting, a chart-topping score can be misleading, and the test may not match your actual task anyway. Treat leaderboards with “healthy skepticism,” use them only to build a short list, and then prototype the finalists on your own data before you commit.

Which model for which job?

Pulling it together, the decision always comes back to your requirements. This tree sketches the kind of reasoning the day builds toward — the branches are examples of what might matter most, not a fixed rule.

Recap
  • There is no single best LLM — only the best one for your task. Narrow the field in two stages: the basics first, then the detailed metrics.
  • The basics include open vs closed source, release date and knowledge cutoff, parameters, training tokens, context window, the three kinds of cost, rate limits, speed vs latency, and license.
  • Closed/frontier models win on build cost and time to market; open models you host can win on cost per call and give you more control, at the price of more work.
  • The Chinchilla scaling law: parameters and training data grow together — double one, roughly double the other (e.g. 8B → 16B).
  • Benchmarks are useful but can mislead: inconsistent reporting, narrow scope, training-data leakage, overfitting, and possible evaluation awareness.
  • Never choose on a leaderboard alone — use it to build a short list, then prototype the finalists on your own data.
