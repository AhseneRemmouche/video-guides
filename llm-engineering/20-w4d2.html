What a leaderboard is

A leaderboard is a public web page that scores many models against the same tests and ranks them from best to worst. Each one runs a fixed set of benchmarks — standard exams for models — and reports a number per model, so you can line them up side by side. Most of the leaderboards in this lesson live on Hugging Face as "Spaces" (small hosted apps), but some are run by other companies. Because every board chooses its own tests, each one answers a slightly different question: how clever is the model, how fast is it, how cheap is it, how good is it at code or medicine?

New to this? What exactly is a leaderboard?

Think of a leaderboard like a sports league table. Every model plays the same set of "matches" (the benchmark tests), gets a score, and is ranked. The value for you is that the comparison is apples to apples: all the models faced the same questions under the same rules, so the ranking means something. A leaderboard does not tell you which model is right for your project — only how models did on its particular tests. Your job is to pick the board whose tests look most like your real problem, then read the ranking there.

Today adds one new idea on top of ordinary leaderboards. Most boards score models automatically with a benchmark. The Chatbot Arena does something completely different: it lets humans decide, by chatting with two anonymous models and voting for the better reply. We will meet it after the six leaderboards.

The six essential leaderboards

The instructor walks through six boards worth bookmarking. The first few live on Hugging Face; the last two are run by other companies and mix open-source and closed (commercial) models together. Each uses its own evaluation metrics, so read what each one actually measures before trusting its ranking.

  1. Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard. The one you already met on Day 1. It ranks open-source models on general skills and now has a newer, harder version (tougher tests, because models kept maxing out the old ones). It is the go-to place to compare open-source models.
  2. BigCode Models Leaderboard. A Hugging Face board for models built to write code. It scores them on coding tests across several programming languages — Python, Java, JavaScript and C++ — so you can see which model is strongest in the language you care about. A "win rate" column is roughly an average across those languages. If your problem is coding, this is your board.
  3. LM Perf Leaderboard. Another Hugging Face board, but about a different dimension entirely: performance in the engineering sense — how fast a model generates text, how much memory and energy it uses, and therefore how expensive it is to run. Its "Find your best model" view plots speed, accuracy and memory footprint together, so you can hunt for a model that is fast, accurate and small enough for your hardware. Essential when you plan to run an open-source model yourself.
  4. Domain and language boards. Hugging Face hosts many narrower boards. The instructor shows an Open Medical LLM board scored on medical tests (clinical knowledge, college biology, medical genetics, PubMed questions), and mentions language-specific boards (for example Portuguese). If your use case is a particular field or language, there may be a board built just for it.
  5. Vellum LLM Leaderboard. Run by the company Vellum, and not on Hugging Face. Its strength is that it brings open-source and closed models together in one place, with easy comparison charts and — crucially — a table of API cost and context-window size per model, kept up to date. That cost-and-context table is one of the few reliable single-page summaries, which is why it is worth bookmarking.
  6. Seal Leaderboard. Run by Scale (scale.com), a company that builds custom datasets. Seal is really a set of boards for specific expert skills and business tasks — coding, instruction-following, maths, adversarial robustness (resisting prompts designed to trigger harmful answers), and particular languages. New boards are added over time, so it keeps growing.
What are "specialized" leaderboards, and why so many?

A specialized leaderboard narrows the tests to one field, task or language instead of general ability. A medical board asks medical questions; a coding board asks programming questions; a Portuguese board tests Portuguese. Why bother? Because a model that tops the general board may be mediocre at your specific job, and a smaller, focused model may quietly win there. If you are building a medical assistant, the medical board tells you far more than the general one. The rule of thumb: the closer a board's tests are to your real problem, the more its ranking is worth to you.

The LMSYS Chatbot Arena

The last resource is not a normal leaderboard at all — it is an arena. Instead of running fixed benchmark tests, the LMSYS Chatbot Arena asks humans to judge. It is a crowdsourced, open platform that has collected more than a million human votes, and it focuses on one thing: how good a model is at chatting — following instructions and holding a natural conversation.

Here is how a vote works. You type one prompt. It is sent to two anonymous models, called only Model A and Model B — you are not told which is which. You read both replies, decide which you prefer, and vote. Only after you vote are the two model names revealed. Because the test is blind, you cannot be swayed by a brand name; you judge the answer on its own merits.

What is the Chatbot Arena?

The Chatbot Arena (run by a group called LMSYS) is a website where ordinary people help rank chat models by voting. You ask a question, two hidden models answer, you pick the better one, and your vote is added to millions of others. Over all those votes, patterns emerge: some models win far more often than others. It is popular because it is fun, it is hands-on, and — unlike a benchmark — it measures the thing people actually care about: "which model do humans prefer to talk to?" The instructor encourages you to go vote yourself; every vote is useful data given back to the community.

Elo ratings: ranking from head-to-head battles

With a million one-on-one votes, how do you turn all those small contests into a single ranking? The arena uses an Elo rating, the same system used to rank chess players. Each model has a number. When two models meet and one wins the vote, the winner's number goes up a little and the loser's goes down — and beating a strong model earns more than beating a weak one. After enough battles, each model settles at a rating that reflects how it does against all its peers. A higher Elo means humans prefer it more often; the gap between two ratings tells you how decisively.

What is an Elo rating?

Elo (named after its inventor, Arpad Elo) is a way to score players from the results of head-to-head games; chess has used it for decades. Every player starts with a number. Win, and your number rises; lose, and it falls — by more when you beat someone rated far above you, by less when you beat someone weak. The clever part is that you never need every player to face every other: from enough matches, the ratings line everyone up correctly. The Chatbot Arena treats each pair of models like two chess players and each human vote like a game result, so the same maths produces a league table of models.

One handy extra: because the arena lists each model alongside details like its training knowledge cut-off date, it doubles as a convenient place to look those up in one spot.

How to use these in practice

With so many boards, the trick is knowing which to open for a given question. Match the resource to what you care about: the task, the speed and cost, or the feel of the conversation.

How to get value from leaderboards.
  • Pick the board that matches your task — coding work → BigCode; a medical product → the medical board; running a model yourself → LM Perf for speed and memory.
  • Use Vellum for the money questions. When you need API cost and context-window size in one place, and want open and closed models on the same chart, open Vellum.
  • Use the arena for the human question. If what matters is whether people enjoy the model's replies, the Chatbot Arena's human votes capture that better than any automatic test.
  • Read the "About" page. Each board explains which datasets it uses and how scores are calculated — that context is what makes a ranking trustworthy.
  • Bookmark them. Models change constantly; these pages stay current so you can re-check whenever you start a new project.
Leaderboards are a starting point, not the final word (back to Day 1).
  • A benchmark can be "gamed" or leaked. As Day 1 warned, a model can score high because its training data overlapped with the test (contamination), not because it is genuinely better. A top rank is evidence, not proof.
  • The board's test may not match your use case. A model that wins a general board can still be weak on your specific task. Prefer a specialized board, and treat general scores with a pinch of salt — the instructor says exactly this about the much-quoted MMLU metric.
  • Human votes are subjective too. The arena measures what people prefer, which is not the same as what is correct or safe for your product.
  • So don't pick one model from a ranking. Use the boards to choose two or three candidates, then prototype with your own data before committing. Numbers narrow the field; your own test picks the winner.

Leaderboard versus arena

It is worth pinning down the difference, because it shapes what each tells you. A leaderboard runs fixed tests and scores them automatically; it is repeatable and objective but only measures what its tests cover. The arena uses live humans voting blind; it captures real preference and conversation quality but is subjective and limited to the chat use case. You want both: the leaderboard for measurable skills, the arena for human feel.

Commercial use cases: LLMs in the wild

To close, the instructor gives a quick "flyby" of five companies applying language models to real business problems — a reminder of why choosing the right model matters, and food for thought for your own ideas. As you read, imagine which leaderboard you would use to pick a model for each.

  1. Law — Harvey. Tools for lawyers that answer legal questions and comb through legal documents for key terms. Language and nuance are the whole job, so law is a natural fit for LLMs.
  2. Talent and recruitment — Nebula.io. The instructor's own company. It uses LLMs to understand people's careers, helping managers hire and engage candidates and helping people find work where they will be satisfied.
  3. Legacy code — Bloop. A platform that ports old legacy code (such as COBOL) into Java. A perfect job for a code model that understands many languages, and one the instructor admits he wishes he had thought of.
  4. Healthcare — Salesforce Einstein Copilot Health Actions. A dashboard that can, for example, summarize the outcome of a medical appointment for a care coordinator, saving time on paperwork.
  5. Education — Khanmigo (Khan Academy). An LLM-based companion for teachers, learners and parents. Applying LLMs to education, the instructor says, is "an absolute no-brainer."
This connects to the week's project. Next up is the week's challenge: a tool that converts Python into fast C++ using a frontier model and an open-source model, then compares the results. Picking the right models for that job is exactly what today's leaderboards and the BigCode coding board are for — so you start the project already knowing where to look.
Recap
  • A leaderboard scores many models on the same fixed benchmarks and ranks them; most of today's live on Hugging Face as Spaces, two are run by other companies.
  • The six to bookmark: HF Open LLM (open-source general skills), BigCode (coding, many languages), LM Perf (speed, memory, cost), domain/language boards (such as medical), Vellum (open + closed, with a cost and context-window table) and Seal (expert and business skills).
  • The Chatbot Arena is not a benchmark but an arena: humans chat with two anonymous models, vote blind, and models are ranked by an Elo rating from more than a million votes.
  • Elo comes from chess — each head-to-head result nudges two numbers, and across many games everyone lines up correctly; higher Elo means humans prefer that model more often.
  • Match the resource to your need, but remember Day 1's warning: benchmarks can be gamed or leaked and may not fit your use case, so use these to shortlist two or three models, then prototype on your own data before choosing.
  • LLMs are already commercial across law (Harvey), talent (Nebula.io), code (Bloop), healthcare (Salesforce) and education (Khanmigo) — and the right board helps you pick a model for each.
