From a business problem to production: the five-step strategy

Before writing any model code, the course lays out a five-step path that works for almost any commercial LLM project. Our own project - predicting a product's price from its description - runs through exactly these steps, and so will the projects you build after the course.

  • 1. Understand. Gather the business requirements. Decide up front how success is measured - not just the data-science metric, but the business metric your users and sponsors care about. Dig into the data: how much is there, how good is it, is it structured or unstructured? Pin down the non-functional requirements too: budget, how far it must scale, latency (how long a user will wait for an answer), and time to market. A tight deadline pushes you toward a frontier model behind an API and a quick UI such as Gradio.
  • 2. Prepare. Research what already solves this today, including non-AI solutions (even a few if statements) and traditional data-science models like linear regression. Those give you a baseline - a starting score you can prove you beat. Compare the relevant models on price, context length, licensing and benchmarks. Then curate, scrub and split the data.
  • 3. Select. Choose the model or handful of models, using the leaderboard and comparison work from earlier weeks.
  • 4. Customize. Squeeze more out of the chosen model with one of the big techniques - RAG or fine-tuning (covered next).
  • 5. Productionize. Turn the notebook into something real (covered further down).
New to "baseline" and "non-functional requirements"? What they mean A baseline is the simplest solution that already works - an existing tool, a rule of thumb, or a basic model like linear regression. You build it first even if you are sure your LLM will crush it, because it gives you a number to beat and lets you show the improvement in a measurable way. Non-functional requirements are the constraints around the task rather than the task itself: budget, scale, latency and deadline. They shape your choices as much as the problem does - a split-second response or a one-week deadline rules out some options before you start.

Three ways to improve results: prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning

Step 4, "customize", is where a lot of confusion lives. There are three main techniques, and people often ask which one to pick. The key split is when each technique acts. Prompting and RAG are inference-time techniques: you take an already-trained model and, at the moment you ask it, feed it better instructions or better context. Fine-tuning is a training-time technique: you supply extra data to tweak the model's own weights so it is permanently better at your task.

New to prompting, RAG and fine-tuning? A one-line each Prompting means getting better answers by writing better messages - adding a system prompt, giving a few worked examples (multi-shot), chaining several calls, or giving the model tools. Nothing about the model changes. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means keeping your facts in a searchable store, pulling the few relevant pieces for each question, and pasting them into the prompt so the model answers from real data. Fine-tuning means training an existing model further on many examples so it learns a skill, a style or deep domain knowledge and carries that in its own weights.

The benefits of each

  • Prompting is fast and cheap. You change the words you send and often see an immediate, direct improvement. Of the three it is the quickest to try.
  • RAG gives strong accuracy because you arm the model with a specific, correct fact. It scales to huge knowledge bases and is efficient: you only pull in the relevant context instead of paying to stuff the whole prompt.
  • Fine-tuning builds deep, nuanced expertise. It can learn a subtle style or tone (for example, emulating customer-service specialists) that a system prompt cannot fully capture, and once trained it is fast at inference - the knowledge is already in the weights, so there is no lookup step as in RAG.

And the costs of each

  • Prompting is limited by the context window. You can only fit so much, and pumping in huge context gives diminishing returns and makes each call slower and more expensive. Prompt chaining (several calls) slows things further.
  • RAG is more of a lift to build: you need a vector database, you must fill it, and you must keep the knowledge base up to date. It also lacks nuance - it hands over facts but does not learn deeper meaning.
  • Fine-tuning is hard. It needs a lot of data and a training cost, and it carries the risk of catastrophic forgetting: train a base model heavily on your narrow task and it can start to forget some of its general knowledge, so quality outside your task may degrade.

Why fine-tuning for the price predictor. The instructor picks fine-tuning here on purpose: we have tons of data, a specialized task, we want top performance, and we want a nuanced understanding of products - enough that the model can tell apart a wide range of prices. That combination is exactly the case fine-tuning is made for.

Productionizing: step five

Step five is the one many people never think about: your model performs well, so now what? A Jupyter notebook does not go to production by itself. Typically you define an API that calls your code (which in turn runs an open-source model, or calls a frontier model wrapped in scaffolding like RAG or prompt engineering). Then you decide how to host and deploy it, how to monitor it, how to handle information security, and how it will scale up. Finally you measure the business metrics you identified back in step one, and you keep retraining, measuring and improving on an ongoing basis.

Where our project stands. Understand - done. Prepare - we are in the middle of it right now, building the dataset. Select, customize and productionize - still to come. The rest of this page finishes the "prepare" step.

Scaling up to a big, balanced dataset

Back in JupyterLab, we extend yesterday's work from one small category to a dataset big enough to move the needle during training. The course ships a tiny helper module, loaders.py, with a single ItemLoader class. It loads one category from the Hugging Face dataset repository and uses Python's concurrent.futures to split the work across several workers (eight by default on the instructor's eight-core machine; pass fewer if your machine is smaller). It loads the data in chunks of 1,000 points at a time. There is no magic here - it is just an efficient way to load many items in parallel.

One decision baked into the loader shapes everything downstream: it keeps only products priced between $0.50 and $999.49. Very expensive outliers are rare and distort results - if the test set happens to include a hugely priced item and the model is off by a bit, the error can be wild. Because the project measures error as the plain difference between predicted and actual price, keeping prices in a sane range keeps the error numbers meaningful. So the scope is "things that cost under $1,000."

With that in place we load far more. The appliances category alone is about 28,625 items in range. Pulling in all the chosen categories - automotive (over 900,000), electronics (over 400,000), tools and home improvement, and more - gives a grand total of just over 2.8 million data points. That is far more than we need, and it is heavily skewed: dominated by cheap items and by the automotive category. We need to fix that skew.

New to "balanced dataset" and "sampling"? Why skew is a problem A dataset is balanced when the things you care about - here, price points and product categories - are reasonably represented, not crowded out by one dominant group. Sampling means deliberately picking a subset instead of using everything. If you train on raw, skewed data, the model mostly sees cheap automotive parts and learns to predict those well while doing poorly on expensive items or other categories. We do not want to erase real-world skew entirely (cheap things really are more common), but we do want to correct it enough that training is not distorted.

The fix is to bucket and sample. The instructor builds a dictionary called slots whose key is every whole-dollar price from 1 to 999, and whose value is the list of all items at that price. A defaultdict(list) makes this tidy: a missing key is auto-created as an empty list, so no if test is needed. Then he walks through all 999 slots and samples from each:

  • For prices above $240, take the whole slot - expensive items are scarce, so keep them all.
  • For cheaper prices, take about 1,200 items per slot using NumPy's np.random.choice.
  • Pass choice a set of weights so some items count for more: automotive gets a weight of 1 and everything else gets 5, nudging the sample away from the automotive flood while staying roughly true to reality.

These exact numbers came from trial and error - tweaking until the resulting histograms looked right - not from a formula. The result is a sample of about 408,000 items (he aimed for roughly 400,000, which is already large for fine-tuning). The price distribution is now far more even across the range, with natural little spikes at marketing prices like $399 and $499 that reflect the real world. The category mix shifts only slightly - automotive still leads, but no longer dominates - which the instructor leaves alone on purpose, because that imbalance is genuinely true of the real world.

Why unbalanced data hurts - and watch for leakage. Train on the raw skewed pile and the model becomes an expert at cheap automotive parts and hopeless at anything expensive, because that is almost all it saw. Balancing fixes that. A second, separate trap is leakage: if information about the answer sneaks into the input, or if the same items end up in both training and test, your scores look great in the notebook and collapse in production. That is why we shuffle and split carefully (next), and why the test set is held out and never used for tuning.

A last look: price vs description length, then the split

One honest question before training: is an item's price correlated with how long its description is? If expensive things always had longer descriptions, a model could cheat by counting words. The course draws a scatter plot of description length (x) against price (y) across the 400,000-point sample. There is a faint hint that longer descriptions trend slightly pricier, plus the familiar spikes at boundary prices, but no strong correlation. That is reassuring: traditional machine learning that leans on description length alone probably will not find an easy shortcut.

The instructor also checks tokens: using the Llama tokenizer, every three-digit number from 1 to 999 maps to a single token (GPT's tokenizer does too; some others split numbers into several tokens). That is a small convenience for training later, not a requirement.

Finally we prepare the split. The sample is sorted cheapest-first because we built it slot by slot, which is useless for training, so we random.shuffle it - after setting a random seed so the shuffle is reproducible and everyone gets the same dataset. Then we take the first 400,000 as the training set and the next 2,000 as the test set. (A data scientist would normally reserve 5-10% for test, and often a separate validation chunk too; here only a few hundred test points are ever needed for accurate results, so 2,000 is plenty for this project.)

New to "train / test split"? Why hold data back You never judge a model on the exact examples it learned from - it could just memorise them. So you split your data: the training set is what the model learns from, and the test set is held out and shown only at the very end to measure how well the model does on data it has never seen. Many projects add a third validation set used while tuning, keeping the test set truly untouched until the final score. The golden rule: no test item may appear in training, or the score is meaningless.
New to the Hugging Face Datasets Hub? What it is The Hugging Face Hub is a public (or private) home for datasets and models. The datasets library turns your Python lists into a Dataset object, groups the train and test parts into a DatasetDict, and push_to_hub(...) uploads it under your username. After that, anyone (including future-you on another machine) can download the exact same data in one line - so the curation work on this page only has to happen once.

Recap
  • The five-step strategy for any LLM project: understand, prepare, select, customize, productionize - each step depends on the one before.
  • Three ways to improve results. Prompting and RAG act at inference time (the model is unchanged); fine-tuning acts at training time (the weights change). Prompting is fast and cheap but limited by context; RAG is accurate and scalable but a bigger build and lacks nuance; fine-tuning gives deep, nuanced skill but needs lots of data and risks catastrophic forgetting.
  • Our price predictor fits fine-tuning: lots of data, a specialized task, top performance and nuance wanted.
  • Productionizing (step five) means an API, hosting, monitoring, security, scaling, measuring business metrics, and continuous retraining - not just a notebook.
  • We scaled to ~2.8M items (prices $0.50-$999.49), then balanced them: bucket by whole-dollar price into slots, keep scarce expensive slots whole, sample ~1,200 from cheap slots with category weights (automotive 1, rest 5), giving a ~408,000-item sample with a far flatter price distribution.
  • We checked that price and description length are not strongly correlated, shuffled with a fixed seed, split into training and test sets, and uploaded the dataset to the Hugging Face Hub for one-line reuse.
  • Honesty: the code needs a Hub login, paid batch access and large downloads, so it was not run live here; the repo has also refactored the hand-built balancing into prepared datasets plus a batch summarization step (see the Video-vs-repo note).
