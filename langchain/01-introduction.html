New to AI apps? What an "agent" is and why it matters

A large language model (LLM) such as OpenAI's gpt-5-nano is a program that reads text and writes text. On its own it can only answer from what it learned during training. It cannot open a file, look at today's news or remember yesterday's chat.

An agent is an LLM placed inside a small loop that lets it do things: it can decide to call a tool (for example "search the web"), read the tool's result, and then decide what to do next, until it has a final answer. That loop is called the agent loop. The personal chef is an agent: the model looks at the photo, decides to search for recipes, reads the results and writes the answer.

You do not need to understand the loop yet. Step 05 builds your first agent and step 08 shows the loop message by message.

The series roadmap (01:08–02:42)

The presenter calls this video a blueprint: a reference you can come back to whenever a later project confuses you. The full plan is split into three videos (beginner, intermediate, advanced), each ending with a project, plus a later one that combines everything. This video, and this guide, cover the beginner phase.

Some words in the intermediate and advanced columns may be new. You do not need them now, but here is a one-line preview of each so the roadmap makes sense:

  • MCP (Model Context Protocol): a standard way to plug ready-made tools and data sources into an agent.
  • Multi-agent systems: several specialised agents that work together, for example one for the venue and one for the menu of a wedding.
  • Middleware: code that runs before or after each model call, for example to shorten a long conversation.
  • Human in the loop: the agent pauses and asks a person to approve an action before it continues.

The last page of the handbook, What's Next, goes deeper on these topics.

How to study (02:42–04:59)

Before any LangChain, the presenter shares the study method they used through their bachelor's and master's degrees. The core idea: learning is a marathon, not a race. If you pour a huge amount of information into your head in one sitting, you keep only a tiny part. If you take in a few ideas at a time, you keep most of them. That is also why the series is split into three videos: you finish one, build its project, and feel the progress before moving on.

The Pomodoro timer

The presenter's second tip is a timer they use themselves: pomofocus.io, a free website that implements the Pomodoro technique. It works like this:

  1. Focus for 25 minutes. One task only, for example "do step 04". No phone, no other tabs.
  2. Take a 5-minute short break. Stand up, drink water, look away from the screen.
  3. Repeat. After a few focus rounds (the presenter says "every two or three times"), the timer gives you a long break. On pomofocus.io the lengths and the number of rounds before a long break are settings you can change.

Why the presenter likes it: when you want to check your phone or feel tired, you know the break is only minutes away, so you keep going. Over a few rounds that builds momentum.

Common mistakes

  • Watching the whole video in one go. It is 1 hour 43 minutes. You will remember far more if you split it into the step pages here.
  • Only watching, never typing. Reading code feels like understanding; running it shows whether you do. Type each file and run it.
  • Skipping the setup step. Almost every error in later steps (missing package, missing key) comes from a setup detail in step 03.
  • Expecting it to be free. LangChain itself is free, but each call to an OpenAI model costs a little money from your credit. Use the small model the presenter uses.
