Rules of engagement: ethics and authorization

The single most important chapter: when security testing is legal, what authorization means, and the line this guide never crosses.

This is the most important page in the guide. Everything else assumes you have read it. Security skills are neutral tools; what makes using them legal or illegal is permission. Get this wrong and good intentions will not keep you out of serious trouble.

The one rule. Only ever test a system you own, or one whose owner has given you explicit, written permission to test, within an agreed scope. There are no exceptions in this guide.

Why this comes first

The source course teaches real offensive techniques alongside defensive ones. Tools like a wordlist scanner, a login tester, or an injection probe are perfectly legal when pointed at your own lab and a crime when pointed at someone else's server — the code is identical. The difference is entirely authorization. So before you run anything, you need to know where the line is.

What counts as authorization

  1. Your own machine and lab. The practice lab in this guide (lab_server.py) runs on 127.0.0.1 — your own computer talking to itself. That is always allowed.
  2. Deliberately vulnerable practice apps you install locally (for example OWASP Juice Shop, DVWA, or similar) — built for exactly this.
  3. Legal Capture the Flag events and hacking labs (HackTheBox, TryHackMe, PortSwigger Web Security Academy) that invite you to attack their targets.
  4. A signed engagement. A penetration test or red-team job with a written contract that names the exact systems and actions in scope.
  5. A bug-bounty program — but only within the rules and scope the program publishes.
Not authorization: "the site looked insecure", "I was only testing", "I did not change anything", "it is a big company, they will not notice", or finding a target in a tutorial. None of these are permission.

The line this guide never crosses

The course includes three techniques that are built to harm. We deliberately do not reproduce weaponised versions of them, and neither should you:

Technique in the videoWhat we do instead
A script that copies saved browser logins and sends them to a Telegram bot (an infostealer)Explain how the theft works and give a read-only check of your own machine plus detection and defence advice (Step 02)
A brute-force password guesser against a login panelShow that a login with no lockout is weak, then build the defence that stops guessing (Step 25)
Exploiting SQL injection and XSS on live targetsDetect the signals on our own lab and give the fix — output encoding and parameterised queries (Steps 13 and 26)

If you find a real vulnerability

Sometimes you will spot a genuine flaw — in your employer's systems, or while using a site normally. The right path is responsible disclosure: do not exploit it, do not tell the world, and do not download other people's data to "prove" it. Report it privately to the owner (or through their security contact or bug-bounty program) and give them time to fix it.

Keep the mindset of a defender. Most of this guide — reading malware, auditing headers, understanding attacks — is blue-team work. Even the offensive chapters exist so you can recognise and stop the real thing. Learn the attack to build the defence.
Recap
  • Authorization, not intention, is what makes testing legal.
  • Your own lab, legal CTFs, and signed engagements are safe places to practise.
  • This guide never ships a working stealer or password guesser; it teaches detection and defence.
  • Find a real bug? Disclose it responsibly and never exploit it.
