Law, disclosure and staying out of trouble

Computer-misuse laws, scope, responsible disclosure and bug-bounty rules.

The skills in this guide are legal to practise and illegal to misuse, and the only thing that separates the two is permission. This chapter explains the general legal landscape around computer misuse, why authorization and scope sit at the centre of everything, and how to handle a real vulnerability through responsible disclosure and bug-bounty rules.

This is general education, not legal advice. Laws differ by country and change over time, and only a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction can advise on your situation. Nothing here is a substitute for that. When in doubt, do not test, and get written permission first.

The shape of computer-misuse law

Almost every country has a law that makes unauthorized access to a computer system a crime. The details vary, but the shared principle is remarkably consistent: it is not about the technique you used or whether you caused damage - it is about whether you had permission to be there at all. Accessing a system, or exceeding the access you were given, without authorization is the core offence.

Two often-cited examples, named here only to illustrate the pattern, not as a complete or current statement of the law:

Example lawJurisdictionCore idea (simplified)
Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)United StatesCriminalises accessing a computer "without authorization" or exceeding authorized access
Computer Misuse ActUnited KingdomMakes unauthorized access, and unauthorized acts that impair a system, offences

Many other countries have close equivalents. The lesson for you is not to memorise statutes but to internalise the shared idea: no permission, no access. Check the laws that apply where you live and where the target is, because both can matter.

Authorization and scope are everything

Authorization is explicit permission from the system's owner to do specific things. Scope is the fence around that permission: which systems, which techniques, which times, and what is off-limits. A professional engagement writes both down before any testing starts, so there is never a question of what was allowed.

New to "scope"? What it is and why it matters

Scope is the agreed boundary of a test. A good scope names the exact hosts, URLs or IP ranges you may touch, the methods you may use (and those you may not, such as denial-of-service), the hours you may test, and who to contact if something breaks. Staying inside scope is what keeps authorized testing authorized - stepping outside it, even by accident, can turn a lawful test into an unauthorized act.

What is not authorization. "The site looked insecure", "I did not change anything", "it was already exposed", "I was just curious", or finding a target in a tutorial - none of these grant permission. Only the owner can, and for anything beyond your own lab you want it in writing.

A penetration test versus poking around

A penetration test is authorized, scoped, documented work done to find and report weaknesses so they can be fixed - typically as part of blue-team defence or a red-team exercise. "Poking around" an interesting site is none of those things, however harmless it feels. The techniques can be identical; the legal and ethical status could not be more different.

Responsible disclosure

Sometimes you will stumble on a genuine flaw - in your employer's systems, or just while using a site normally. Responsible disclosure (also called coordinated disclosure) is the accepted way to handle that: report it privately to the owner and give them reasonable time to fix it before any public mention. The goal is to get the hole closed, not to score points.

Note that finding a flaw by accident does not grant permission to dig further. The moment you realise something is wrong, the responsible move is to stop testing and report - not to see how far the hole goes.

Bug-bounty programs

A bug-bounty program is a standing invitation from an organisation to test specific systems and report vulnerabilities, sometimes for a reward. It is one of the few ways to legally test someone else's systems - but only within the published rules. Those rules are your scope, and they are strict for a reason.

  • Read the program policy first. It lists in-scope targets, out-of-scope targets, and forbidden techniques.
  • Stay inside the listed scope. A subdomain that is not listed is not fair game.
  • Follow the testing limits. Many programs forbid automated scanning, denial-of-service, or social engineering.
  • Report through the right channel and do not disclose publicly until allowed.
A bounty is permission, with conditions. Stepping outside a program's scope - testing a system it does not list, or using a banned technique - can remove the very authorization that made your testing legal. Treat the policy as a contract.

Keeping yourself safe

Good habits protect you as much as they protect targets. Keep written evidence of permission for any engagement. Save the scope document. Log what you did and when. If a test touches personal data, handle it with extra care, because data-protection laws may apply on top of computer-misuse laws. And keep the whole thing boring and professional: the best security work leaves a clear, authorized paper trail.

Recap
  • Computer-misuse laws worldwide share one idea: unauthorized access is an offence, regardless of technique.
  • Examples like the US CFAA and the UK Computer Misuse Act illustrate the pattern; laws vary and change - this is not legal advice.
  • Authorization plus a written scope is what makes testing lawful; "it looked insecure" is never permission.
  • Found a real bug? Use responsible disclosure: report privately, do not exploit, wait for the fix.
  • Bug-bounty programs grant permission only within their published rules and scope.
  • Keep written proof of permission and a clear record of what you did.
